ABSTRACT 

We audit frequency and decoder-depth claims for AdvWave-P, an additive audio jailbreak, on Qwen2-Audio. The protocol masks frequency components of the per-turbation in the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain and measures attack success and audio-span representations. On 520 AdvBench prompts, the primary judge labels 76.7% of adversarial inputs as jailbreaks. A condition-blind, single-annotator validation yields a Rogan-Gladen sensitivity estimate of 0.87 for this adversarial condition (approximately 0.83–0.95 when validation-rate uncertainty is propagated); this correction is not applied to masked conditions. The apparent frequency ranking depends on the partition: energy share alone predicts the stan-dard eight-band ranking (Spearman’s ρ = 0 .95 ), and equal-Hz and equal-energy partitions show that masking any tested band can sharply reduce attack success. At a finer, 16-band equal-energy resolution, however, masking the narrow 7520 –

7960 Hz band leaves ASR at 0.10; without a matched control at this resolution, this remains unresolved. Matched-energy scattered-removal tests show that con-tiguous removal is more damaging in lower bands, while both forms of removal approach the floor in upper bands. Global rescaling leaves ASR near baseline, but tests amplitude sensitivity rather than frequency location. In a re-optimization pi-lot ( n = 20 ), tested single-band and two-band supports reach ASRs of 0.00, 0.25, and 0.40, while random supports covering about half the STFT bins reach a mean of 0.86. A prompt- and energy-adjusted model finds that audio-span divergence is associated with band necessity, with the coefficient increasing from +0 .63 at the projector output to +0 .93 at layer 30 (contrast +0 .293 , 95% CI [0 .11 , 0.51] ). This association is not a causal localization, and single-layer patching does not establish a causal layer. Together, the results support a partition-aware audit of frequency claims while leaving the fine-resolution top-band result and broader generality open. 1 INTRODUCTION 

Audio-language models (ALMs), including Qwen2-Audio and LLaMA-Omni, extend language models with speech frontends (Chu et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025). A model may refuse a harmful text instruction yet comply when the same instruction is presented as adversarial audio. AdvWave and AdvWave-P demonstrate this risk on AdvBench (Kang et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2023); related vulnerabilities have also been reported for other attacks and ALMs (Song et al., 2025; Peri et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025). To evaluate frequency-localization claims, an analysis must distinguish a band’s location from its width, energy, and the structure of the removed signal. Prior work selects frequency regions for different purposes. GRM ranks mel bands by an attack-to-utility gradient ratio (Wang et al., 2026). ALMGuard selects mel bins for applying a defensive universal perturbation, balancing jailbreak mitigation against speech-recognition performance (Jin et al., 2025). Gupta et al. use encoder attention to identify a broad 1–8 kHz region for universal audio jailbreaks (Gupta et al., 2025). These methods do not all make the same claim about which com-1arXiv:2610.07005v1 [cs.SD] 4 Oct 2026 ponents of an attacker’s perturbation are necessary. We test how apparent band importance changes with the analysis partition, perturbation energy, and removal structure, then examine its association with decoder representations. We study AdvWave-P on Qwen2-Audio and use it to audit frequency-localization claims. We mask perturbation components in the STFT domain, reconstruct each waveform, and measure attack suc-cess and decoder representations. The comparisons include random contiguous regions, equal-Hz and equal-energy partitions, matched-energy scattered removal, and global rescaling. The standard eight-band ranking is strongly associated with band energy, while the finer equal-energy sweep leaves one narrow top-frequency result unresolved because it lacks a matched control. Separately, a prompt- and energy-adjusted model finds a depth-varying association between audio-span diver-gence and band necessity. This association does not establish a causal layer. We make four contributions, summarized with their supporting results in Table 1. • A controlled audit of frequency rankings (Section 3). The protocol measures judge-adjudicated ASR and layer-wise audio-span divergence after STFT-domain occlusion. Random-region, equal-Hz, equal-energy, and scattered-removal comparisons expose how band rankings depend on partition and removal structure; global rescaling serves only as an amplitude check. • Frequency results depend on resolution (Section 5.1). Energy share alone closely pre-dicts the standard eight-band ranking (Spearman ρ = 0 .95 ). Equal-Hz and eight-band equal-energy partitions show substantial effects across bands, while the narrow top band in the 16-band equal-energy sweep remains unresolved without a matched control. • Removal structure and support coverage matter (Section 5.2). At low frequencies, con-tiguous removal is more damaging than scattered removal of the same energy; at high frequencies, both approach the floor. In a re-optimization pilot, tested single-band and two-band supports perform poorly, while some random supports covering about half the STFT bins sustain the attack. The ALMGuard and GRM audits are inconclusive at their tested scales and do not evaluate the methods’ original objectives. • A depth-varying representational association (Sections 5.3 and 5.4). In a prompt- and energy-adjusted model, audio-span divergence is associated with band necessity, and the estimated coefficient increases with depth. The association is not causal, and single-layer patching does not identify a causal layer. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Audio jailbreaks on ALMs. Qwen2-Audio and LLaMA-Omni pair a log-mel frontend with an instruction-tuned language model (Chu et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025), but text-side safety does not always transfer to audio. SpeechGuard documents vulnerabilities to adversarial waveforms (Peri et al., 2024). Kang et al. (2025) introduce dual-phase optimization to address gradient shattering in the encoder and report over 90% ASR on AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023). Jin et al. (2025) introduce the ALMGuard defense and AdvWave-P, the additive attack evaluated here. AudioJailbreak and related work broadened evaluation across attacks and ALMs (Song et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). We ask whether apparent frequency patterns persist when bandwidth, perturbation energy, and decoder depth are tested with matched controls. 

Classical audio-adversarial and psychoacoustic attacks. Our analysis also draws on audio-adversarial research. Carlini & Wagner (2018) construct targeted audio attacks. Qin et al. (2019); Sch¨ onherr et al. (2019) use psychoacoustic methods to reduce audibility. These studies motivate attention to spectral structure, but AdvWave-P constrains only ∥δ∥∞ and has no explicit masking-threshold objective. Our findings concern how its spectrum is analyzed, not the psychoacoustic mechanism. 

Frequency-domain structure and defenses. GRM ranks mel bands by attack-to-utility ratio and perturbs a selected subset (Wang et al., 2026). ALMGuard selects mel bins for its defensive uni-versal perturbation (Jin et al., 2025), while Gupta et al. (2025) use encoder attention to identify a broad 1–8 kHz region for universal audio jailbreaks. Yang et al. (2019) study temporal-dependency-based detection, not a band-stop defense. Our analysis tests how frequency rankings change with the partition and removal control, then examines their association with decoder representations. 

Mechanistic interpretability and safety evaluation. We use representation-space probes and lin-ear CKA to examine decoder states (Alain & Bengio, 2017; Kornblith et al., 2019). Appendix A.3 discusses the patching protocol and its limits, in-cluding the single-layer caveat described in recent interpretability guidance (Heimersheim & Nanda, 2024). 

Removal-based attribution and its pitfalls. Occlusion attributes an output change to the region that was removed, but the effect can depend on how the removal is constructed. In vision, Zeiler and Fergus (Zeiler & Fergus, 2014) measure output changes after occluding input regions, while ROAR (Hooker et al., 2019) retrains on the modified data to separate feature information from removal effects. Sanity checks have also shown that some saliency maps can be insensitive to what a model has learned (Adebayo et al., 2018); RISE (Petsiuk et al., 2018) addresses the variability of any single mask by averaging over random masks. Covert et al. (Covert et al., 2021) place these methods within a broader account of removal-based explanations. We apply the same caution to spectral occlusion: our random-band, equal-energy, and scattered-removal controls test whether a measured effect depends on band location, energy, or removal structure. The limits of single-layer patching are discussed in Section 5.4.

We study one audio jailbreak by removing selected frequency components of its perturbation and measuring attack behavior and decoder representations. Behavioral importance measures how each removal changes attack success. We validate the primary judge on 220 condition-blind, human-labeled responses: 120 adversarial responses, 40 clean controls, and 60 responses sampled from disagreement strata. One author an-notated the set; a second annotator was unavailable (Section 7). Appendix A.3.2 gives the sample composition, seed, and blind key. On the 120 adversarial responses, the judge’s false-positive rate is 0.095 (2/21 human negatives; Wilson 95% CI [0 .027 , 0.289] ), and its false-negative rate is 0.131 

(13 /99 human positives; Wilson 95% CI [0 .078 , 0.212] ). Applying the Rogan-Gladen correction to the n = 520 judged adversarial ASR of 0.767 gives approximately 0.87 ; propagating the endpoint confidence intervals gives approximately 0.83 –0.95 . These error rates were estimated only for ad-versarial responses, so we do not apply the correction to masked conditions. For example, applying the adversarial false-positive rate to the judged b8 ASR of 0.05 gives a negative corrected rate. We therefore report the uncorrected judge scores for condition-level comparisons and treat the corrected adversarial estimate as a sensitivity analysis. The primary judge uses the AdvWave LLM-as-a-judge prompt, its default threshold, and GPT-4o-mini at temperature 0 (Section 4).

4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Victim models, attack, and corpus. We evaluate Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct (Chu et al., 2024) (Whisper-large-v3 encoder, L=32 , dllm =4096 ) and LLaMA-Omni (Fang et al., 2025). Both models use a 128 -bin log-mel frontend, distinct from the Qwen analysis basis described in Section 3.1. We attack 520 prompts from AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023) with AdvWave-P (Jin et al., 2025), the ad-ditive variant of AdvWave (Kang et al., 2025). The perturbation is applied to the full waveform; its budget and optimization settings appear in Section 3.1 and Figure 1. The primary K=8 mask and keep-only sweeps and the depth estimators use all 520 prompts. The K=16 behavioral sweep also uses 520 prompts, but its hidden-state depth estimate uses n=50 . Several secondary controls, in-cluding equal-Hz and equal-energy partitions, scattered removal, and global rescaling, are reported at their respective sample sizes and are described separately from the primary-judge results. The ep-silon and resolution-ladder analyses use n=50 . Main sweeps use greedy decoding and one response per prompt and condition. Stochastic-decoding results are a sensitivity check, not headline ASR (Appendix A.7). The mask and keep-only sweeps are separate, each with K+2 conditions: clean, adversarial, and K masked or retained variants. Representation visualizations, the Omni arm, and the transfer matrix use the conditions specified in their captions. Omni’s clean ASR leaves limited headroom for measuring per-band effects. For transfer, each source perturbation is passed unchanged to the target, and source bands are swept in each cell of the 2 × 2 ASR matrix. 

Judge. For the primary K=8 results, we use the AdvWave LLM-as-a-judge prompt (Kang et al., 2025), its default threshold, and GPT-4o-mini at temperature 0. The provider did not expose an immutable model snapshot; the main n = 520 scores were collected through 2026-09-02, and per-sample responses and scores are needed to reproduce these labels if the model changes. Equal-Hz and equal-energy partitions, scattered removal, graded attenuation, global rescaling, and the ALM-Guard suppression sweep were scored with Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct. The GRM re-optimization pilot uses GPT-4o-mini. Each result is interpreted within its scorer-specific run, and ASRs from different judges are not pooled. We also scored the n = 50 K=8 sensitivity set with the propri-etary reference model GPT-5.6-sol (scored 2026-09-05); this provider model label has no archived immutable snapshot. The reference scores appear in Table 3 and are a sensitivity analysis, not the headline ASR. The clean-audio renderer is described in Appendix A.4. 

5 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

On Qwen2-Audio, the clean-input ASR is 0.008 and the adversarial ASR is 0.767 across the 520 AdvBench prompts. The condition-blind judge validation implies a corrected adversarial estimate of about 0.87 , with a wide interval of approximately 0.83 –0.95 ; we do not transport this correction to masked conditions. Unless noted otherwise, the primary behavioral results use GPT-4o-mini, and the 95% intervals are prompt-bootstrap intervals. Secondary control sweeps and their scorers are identified in Section 4. 6 CONCLUSION 

We audited AdvWave-P on Qwen2-Audio using frequency occlusion and decoder representations. The standard eight-band ranking is closely predicted by band energy, and its apparent location changes with the analysis partition. A finer equal-energy sweep singles out a narrow band near Nyquist, but the absence of a matched control leaves that result unresolved. Matched-energy scattered-removal tests show that contiguous removal is more damaging in lower bands; at higher bands, both conditions approach the floor. Global rescaling leaves ASR near baseline, but it is an amplitude check rather than a frequency-location control. In re-optimization pilots, tested single-band supports and their union perform poorly, while random supports covering about half the STFT bins reach mean ASR 0.86 . The ALMGuard and GRM audits are inconclusive and do not evaluate those methods’ original objectives. A prompt- and energy-adjusted model estimates a depth-varying association between audio-span divergence and band necessity, but the raw depth trend is partition-sensitive and single-layer patching does not establish a causal location. Overall, the results motivate partition-aware controls and leave open whether the findings generalize to other attacks, victims, or perturbation domains. 

7 LIMITATIONS 

Scope. The positive findings come from one attack, AdvWave-P at ϵ = 0 .01 , on one victim with a usable margin, Qwen2-Audio. LLaMA-Omni already judges 40% of clean harmful prompts as jailbroken, leaving too little headroom to measure per-band effects. The protocol is designed for additive perturbations aligned with the clean waveform; it does not directly apply to suffix-appended, semantic, or mel-domain attacks. 8Perturbation budget. Attacks optimized at ϵ = 0 .005 and 0.02 were not stable under cold-start re-inference. Their ASRs fell from 0.84 and 0.76 during optimization to 0.32 and 0.40 at inference on n = 50 prompts; we have not isolated the cause. All behavioral results therefore use ϵ = 0 .01 .The untrained-ϵ probe detects the perturbation family, not necessarily a successful jailbreak. 

Occlusion target. We remove components from the perturbation while holding the clean wave-form fixed. This isolates perturbation structure but does not test a deployable filter, which would have to process the clean-plus-adversarial mixture. The notch result in Appendix A.8 should be read as a perturbation analysis, not as a validated defense. 

Removal displacement. Global rescaling preserves the perturbation’s direction but does not match the band mask’s displacement. For a band with energy share ek, the mask’s relative L2 dis-placement is √ek (0.27 –0.49 ), whereas the rescaling displacement is 1 − √1 − ek (0.037 –0.13 ), 3.8–7.3 times smaller (Table 5). We therefore treat rescaling only as an amplitude check. The com-parison of removal structure relies on the scattered control, which matches both removed energy and bin count. 

Equal-energy resolution. In the K = 8 equal-energy partition, residual ASR ranges from 0.07 

to 0.16 at n = 520 . This spread is several per-band standard errors, so the result supports the claim that masking any band disrupts the attack, not that all bands are equally important. At K = 16 , the narrow near-Nyquist band b15 = [7520 , 7960] Hz leaves residual ASR 0.10 and carries the largest energy share. Without a matched random control at this resolution, we treat this as an unresolved observation, not a localization result. 

Depth. The adjusted depth result is an association, not a causal localization. The raw correla-tion uses only eight bands; its prompt-bootstrap interval excludes zero, but the leave-one-band-out spread is 0.172 , about twice the bootstrap standard error, and the phase-randomized null test gives 

p = 0 .183 . These raw-curve results are conditional on the partition and do not test the adjusted model. The prompt-by-band model adjusts for prompt identity and per-prompt energy; its layer-30-minus-projector contrast is positive, while the tested behavior-irrelevant statistics show no corre-sponding depth contrast. Those controls use statistics defined on the same coarse partition. Single-layer activation patching cannot test effects that develop cumulatively across layers, so the causal location remains unresolved. Cumulative or KV-cache patching, a refusal-direction readout (Arditi et al., 2024), and a check for massive-activation channels (Sun et al., 2024) are possible next steps. 

Judge. We validated GPT-4o-mini’s AdvWave-judge labels against one author-annotator on a condition-blind sample; a second annotator was unavailable. Applying the Rogan-Gladen correction to the adversarial condition gives 0.87 , with an approximate interval of 0.83 –0.95 after propagating uncertainty in the validation rates. It cannot be transferred to masked conditions: applying the ad-versarial false-positive rate of 0.095 to the judged b8 ASR of 0.05 , for example, yields a negative corrected rate. We therefore report uncorrected scores for condition-level comparisons. The Wilson 95% CIs are [0 .03 , 0.29] for the false-positive rate ( 2/21 ) and [0 .08 , 0.21] for the false-negative rate (13 /99 ). The n=50 reference-judge sensitivity check uses GPT-5.6-sol, a provider model label for which no immutable snapshot was archived; it gives adversarial ASR 0.64 versus 0.76 under the primary judge. The ranking agreement on this sensitivity subset does not remove uncertainty about absolute ASR or about judge-version drift. 

Cross-corpus analyses. These studies use a different TTS renderer. Only the JailbreakBench 

K=8 mask sweep covers its full jailbroken pool ( n = 70 ); MaliciousInstruct and StrongREJECT use the 50 highest-necessity prompts from their respective jailbroken pools. The selected subsets cannot independently validate the ranking used to select them. Depth curves for all three corpora use the existing 50-prompt highest-necessity samples, not the full JailbreakBench pool, and none rejects the phase-randomized null. Re-optimized sufficiency and the GRM support comparison are small pilots ( n = 20 ); in the GRM audit, random supports are scattered and are not structurally matched to the selected support (Appendix A.10). 

ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS 

Use and release of resources. We apply the public AdvWave-P method to public harmful-prompt corpora and public victim models; we introduce no new attack method, prompt corpus, or model. The optimized adversarial waveforms and perturbations are not included in the release. Instead, the repository will provide optimization and analysis code, seeds, hyperparameters, renderer details, prompt identifiers, responses, and judge scores to support re-derivation without distributing those waveforms (Appendix A.5). The analysis is intended to inform defense, though frequency-level findings may also help characterize existing attacks. We recruited no external participants and trained no new models. A.6 GRADIENT BAND -A TTRIBUTION BASELINE 

Occlusion requires K+2 forward passes per sample, so gradient attribution offers a cheaper ranking check. On 50 saved ε = 0 .01 adversarial waveforms, we backpropagate AdvWave-P’s next-token cross-entropy objective to δ and sum squared gradients within each analysis band in the STFT domain. Without re-optimization or generation, this gradient ranking correlates with the occlusion ranking (Spearman ρ = 0 .97 , p < 0.001 ; Pearson 0.84 ). Energy share alone also recovers much of the ranking at n = 520 (Spearman ρ = 0 .95 ). These baselines show that occlusion is not an independent source of the ranking; its added value is the set of controls for bandwidth, energy, and removal structure. Using gradient saliency as the behavioral target also produces a raw depth curve that rises from ρ0 = 0 .70 to ρ30 = 0 .94 (late-decoder slope +0 .0102 per layer). This curve tracks band geometry and is not evidence for a behavior-specific depth effect. A.7 DECODING AND OPTIMIZATION SEED STABILITY 

Decoding seeds. The main sweep uses greedy decoding. To check sensitivity to decoding, we re-evaluate the adversarial condition and the highest- and lowest-importance masked bands with stochastic decoding ( do sample=true , t=1 .0, three seeds, n=520 , cold start). Adversarial ASR varies from 0.56 to 0.75 (mean 0.67 ), below the greedy result of 0.77 . The tested band contrast is consistent across seeds: masking the top band [70 , 79] leaves ASR at 0.04 –0.11 (mean 0.07 ), while masking the low band [0 , 9] leaves ASR at 0.18 –0.31 (mean 0.24 ). These two conditions do not establish a full-band ranking under stochastic decoding. The variation in adversarial ASR is larger than expected from binomial noise alone (per-seed standard error at n=520 is at most 0.022 ); its source remains unresolved. We therefore treat this check as evidence about these two masks, not as a replication of the complete ranking. 

Optimization seeds. We also tested whether the perturbation’s energy profile depends on its ran-dom initialization. On 20 prompts, we re-optimized AdvWave-P at ε = 0 .01 for 3,000 Adam itera-tions from three independent initializations. Every prompt placed its largest energy share in the same band, b8 = [70 , 79] (20/20 prompts). Across seeds, the mean pairwise Spearman correlation of per-band energy shares was 0.97 (minimum 0.88 ); 19 of 20 prompts had all three pairwise correlations at or above 0.9. The largest band’s energy share averaged 0.21 (SD 0.015 ), and every perturbation respected the L∞ bound. This indicates that the top-energy band is stable across these optimization seeds. It does not establish frequency-specific necessity, since the band’s larger frequency width also contributes to its energy share (Section 5.1). 14 A.8 AN ARBITRARY SINGLE NOTCH AS A DEFENSE HYPOTHESIS 

A single band-stop notch can substantially reduce attack success even when applied to a low-frequency band. Masking b1=[0 , 9] , which contains about 10% of the perturbation energy and is the second-least-important band in the n=520 sweep, reduces ASR from 0.77 to 0.24 (n=520 ); the 

n=50 subset drops from 0.76 to 0.34 . This is a property of the tested perturbation, not a validated defense. A practical filter would have to act on the mixture x+δ, and a broad low-frequency notch could also remove speech fundamentals. We did not measure speech utility and therefore do not recommend this intervention. A.9 RESOLUTION LADDER ACROSS K

A.10 AUDIT OF NAMED PRIOR BAND -S ELECTIVITY CLAIMS 

GRM ranks mel bands by an attack-to-utility ratio and perturbs a selected subset (Wang et al., 2026). ALMGuard selects mel bins for applying a defensive universal perturbation, balancing jailbreak mitigation with speech-recognition performance (Jin et al., 2025). Neither selection rule is, by itself, a claim about which components of an attacker’s perturbation are necessary. We applied the selected supports to AdvWave-P as an exploratory audit using random controls. The ALMGuard comparison used equal-energy suppression; the GRM comparison used random supports matched in bandwidth. Both pilots were inconclusive and neither evaluates the methods’ original objectives. 

ALMGuard (equal-energy suppression, n=520 ). We map ALMGuard’s released 128 -mel selec-tion mask for Qwen2-Audio to the 201 -bin STFT analysis basis. We suppress the selected coeffi-cients so that the removed perturbation energy is approximately 10% , then compare with 20 random masks matched on removed energy and bandwidth. The ALMGuard condition gives ASR 0.15 ; ran-dom masks have mean 0.17 and empirical range [0 .150 , 0.187] . Its result falls inside this range, so the pilot does not resolve a difference. This comparison tests the selected mask on AdvWave-P; it is not an evaluation of ALMGuard’s defensive universal perturbation. 

GRM (band-restricted re-optimization, n=20 ). We reproduce GRM’s selection rule, not its orig-inal perturbation, by restricting a newly optimized AdvWave-P perturbation to its selected 109 of 201 STFT bins. Cold-start inference on 20 jailbroken JailbreakBench prompts, scored with GPT-4o-mini, gives ASR 0.75 (15 /20 ). Twenty random supports matched in bandwidth reach mean ASR 

0.86 (range [0 .75 , 0.95] ; bootstrap 95% CI for the mean [0 .84 , 0.885] ). The GRM support lies at the lower edge of the random range. Given this sample size and support construction, the result is inconclusive; the uniformly sampled random supports are scattered and not structurally matched to a contiguous support. Neither pilot resolves whether the selected supports differ from the corresponding random controls. The ALMGuard comparison matches removed energy and bandwidth; the GRM pilot matches band-width but uses scattered random supports, unlike the selected support’s structure. These exploratory tests do not evaluate the original methods or show that their selections are ineffective. They only test the selected regions on AdvWave-P under the stated conditions. For MaliciousInstruct and StrongREJECT, the available analyses use the 50 highest-necessity prompts selected from the jailbroken pools (93/100 and 190/276, respectively). We follow the primary proto-col with Qwen2-Audio, GPT-4o-mini at temperature 0, and float32 reconstruction without clipping; the sample selection differs across these analyses.
