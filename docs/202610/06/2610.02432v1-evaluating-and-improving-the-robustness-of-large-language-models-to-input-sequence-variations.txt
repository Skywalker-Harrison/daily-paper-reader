# Evaluating and Improving the Robustness of Large Language Models to Input Sequence Variations 

> PhD Thesis Narek Maloyan
> Advisor: Dmitry Namiot
> 2026 arXiv:2610.02432v1 [cs.CR] 1 Oct 2026

Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) embedded in production systems face prompt injections, trojans, and manipulation of automatic quality metrics. This thesis develops a set of models, methods, and algorithms for evaluating and improving the robustness of LLMs to adversarial input sequence variations: prompt injection, trojans (backdoor attacks), and attacks on agentic LLM systems. We propose Rstab (f ), a formal metric of generative robustness based on bounded divergence measures, in particular the Jensen–Shannon divergence, between per-step output distributions under small input perturbations. For localized attacks, we prove that the vulnerability of a deterministic decision operator h is bounded by its non-robustness: V (h) ≤ 1 − Rclass (h), where 

Rclass (h) is the expected probability that h preserves its decision under small input perturbations. For non-localized attacks, we propose an empirical calibrated model. For LLM-as-a-Judge systems, we separate instructive and content attacks and develop ASA, an adaptive evolutionary attack that combines token-level mutations with semantic paraphrasing. Under black-box access, ASA reaches an attack success rate (ASR) of up to 73.8%, and the transfer success rate between open models reaches 62.6%. On Trojan Detection Challenge 2023 data for Pythia-1.4B, we quantify an asymmetry of trojan detection: surrogate triggers reach a reverse-engineered attack success rate (REASR) of ≈ 0.99, while the recall of the true triggers is ≈ 0.17 against a baseline of ≈ 0.14. On SaTML CTF 2024, we systematize four classes of bypasses of multi-layer defenses. Committees of 5–7 heterogeneous models reduce the ASR for Gemma-3-4B by 47–55 percentage points, from 73.8% to 19.3% with 7 models. The Condorcet jury theorem justifies the growth of committee accuracy when the members err independently and each has an accuracy above 0.5. For Gemma-3-4B, which does not meet this condition, the gain comes from the heterogeneity of the committee. Multi-layer defense reduces the ASR from 90% to 15–25%. For agentic systems based on the Model Context Protocol (MCP), we propose AttestMCP, which attests tool calls with HMAC-protected packets at an overhead below 0.1 ms per call, and the Commit Boundary isolation pattern. On the MCPBench benchmark of 847 scenarios, our defenses reduce the average ASR from 53.7% to 12.4%. The methods are implemented in the registered software suites JudgeGuard and TrojanArmor and in the MCPSec module. JudgeGuard is deployed in production at Viasat. Contents

3Introduction 

Motivation. Modern large language models (LLMs) solve a wide range of natural language processing tasks. As LLMs are embedded into production systems, the number of entry points through which an adversary can algorithmically influence the model also grows: prompt injections, trojans (backdoors), and manipulation of automatic quality metrics. Among such metrics, the LLM-as-a-Judge architecture, in which a language model evaluates the responses of other models, plays a special role. A compromised judge model distorts model comparisons and undermines the reproducibility of experiments. The search for malicious instructions is an optimization problem over a discrete space of texts. Adversaries iterate over candidate jailbreaks until the model outputs prohibited information. Multi-level heuristic filters do not solve the problem. Trojans work differently: at the training stage, a trigger is injected into the data, and this trigger forms a hidden attractor in the parameter space. Reconstruction of such a trigger from the network weights is an ill-conditioned inverse problem. In the Trojan Detection Challenge 2023, the recall of true-trigger reconstruction was 0.17, which is close to the baseline level. Agentic architectures aggravate the situation: when an LLM gains access to external tools, a textual vulnerability carries over into the space of executable actions. The consequences of an attack then extend beyond the information domain. Experimental evaluation quantitatively confirms the vulnerability of the LLM-as-a-Judge architecture. On the MT-Bench dataset and on the tasks of the Kaggle competition “LLMs: You Can’t Please Them All”, injection attacks generated by the adaptive ASA algorithm reached an attack success rate (ASR) of 73.8% against the Gemma-3-4B-Instruct judge model. The built-in safeguards of judge models fail under targeted attacks. The compositional nature of agentic systems gives rise to a separate class of risks. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes the interaction of an LLM agent with external tools and thereby expands the agent’s state space. An injection into the context of a tool call can take over control of the agent ( prompt injection to tool hijacking ). From an algorithmic point of view, the robustness problem of a single model extends to the computation graph of an entire system: input sequences are formed dynamically from different sources, and an attack through an external channel becomes a special case of an adversarial input perturbation that can provoke unauthorized code execution. All these vulnerabilities have a common cause: the mapping learned by the model is sensitive to small input variations in the discrete token space. Perturbations destabilize hidden representations, and the model loses its intended properties. The embedding of LLMs into complex agentic systems makes the problem more acute. A rigorous framework for ensuring robustness is therefore a pressing research problem. 

State of the art. The foundations of robustness analysis for neural networks were laid by Goodfellow et al. [1] and Madry et al. [2] in computer vision. A direct transfer of this framework to LLMs is difficult because the token space is discrete and inference is autoregressive. For the text domain, Zou et al. [3] proposed the GCG method, which generates universal adversarial suffixes. Wallace et al. [4] showed that universal triggers can control generation. Perez et al. [5] systematized prompt injection attacks. Carlini et al. [6] showed that fragments of training data can be extracted directly from a trained model. However, formal metrics for evaluating LLM robustness remain underdeveloped: the existing metrics do not account for the 4discreteness of the token space and the autoregressive character of generation. Zheng et al. [7] proposed the LLM-as-a-Judge architecture for automatic evaluation of the quality of model responses. It spread quickly, but the analysis of its robustness to targeted adversarial attacks remains fragmentary. Trojan detection was studied in the TDC2023 and TrojAI benchmarks. A quantitative characterization of the asymmetry between trigger injection and trigger detection remained an open question before the present work. The security of agentic systems and of protocols such as MCP has received little study. Greshake et al. [8] described indirect prompt injection attacks on LLM-integrated applications and showed that external content can take over control of an agent. However, to the author’s knowledge, no prior publication analyzed the architectural risks of MCP or proposed defense algorithms based on cryptographic attestation of tool calls. The absence of a comprehensive approach to the security of agentic LLM systems motivated the present research. At the start of this work, the literature lacked: (1) a formal robustness metric that accounts for the discreteness and autoregressiveness of LLM generation, (2) a systematic methodology for evaluating the vulnerability of LLM-as-a-Judge, (3) algorithms for defending agentic systems against injections through protocols of interaction with external tools. The work covers several interrelated directions: • vulnerabilities of LLMs to prompt injection attacks in LLM-as-a-Judge architectures and in systems with multi-layer defense; • limitations of existing trojan detection methods; • vulnerabilities of agentic LLM systems that use MCP to interact with external tools, and software tools for defense against control hijacking; • methods for improving the robustness of LLMs to the listed threats and their experimental evaluation. Studying these directions in one work reveals the common structure of LLM vulnerabilities and yields concrete recommendations for developers. 

Scope. The work studies large language models and systems based on them, namely LLM-as-a-Judge and agentic LLM systems with tool access. It develops models, methods, algorithms, and software tools for evaluating vulnerabilities and improving the robustness of LLMs. 

Aim. The aim of the work is to develop a set of models, methods, and algorithms for evaluating and improving the robustness of large language models to adversarial input sequence variations and injection attacks. In this work, input sequence variations comprise prompt injections, trojans ( backdoor attacks ), and attacks on agentic LLM systems. To achieve this aim, the work addresses the following objectives :1. Systematization and classification of threats. Construct a classification of vulnerabilities of large language models to prompt injection attacks that covers specialized architectures, such as LLM-as-a-Judge and systems with multi-layer defense, and establish the limitations of trojan detection methods. 2. Development of the theoretical foundations of evaluation. Develop a formal mathematical model for robustness evaluation ( Rstab (f )) and a consistent set of empirical metrics that account for the discrete nature of the token space and the autoregressive character of generation, including the basic indicators ASR ( Attack Success Rate ), TSR ( Transfer Success Rate ), Recall, and REASR ( Reverse-Engineered ASR ). 3. Construction of adversarial attack methods. Develop adaptive algorithms for generating adversarial attacks (ASA) to test LLM vulnerabilities, including attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge systems in the black-box setting. 54. Development and experimental validation of defense methods. Propose and experimentally study strategies for improving LLM robustness, namely committees of heterogeneous models and multi-layer filtering, on a representative set of models of the main architectural families, both with open weights (Gemma, Llama) and proprietary (GPT, Claude), and on the MT-Bench, TDC2023, and SaTML CTF 2024 datasets. 5. Software implementation and validation. Create software tools for evaluating the security of LLM systems, including agentic systems based on MCP. Validate the attack and defense methods on the TDC2023 and SaTML CTF 2024 benchmarks and in the production environment of Viasat. 

Scientific novelty. 

1. A formal mathematical model for evaluating the robustness of large language models (Rstab (f )) is proposed as a metric of generative robustness. It accounts for the discrete nature of the token space and the autoregressive character of generation. It is based on bounded divergence measures, in particular the Jensen–Shannon divergence, between the per-step output distributions under small input perturbations. The metric is shown to be monotone with respect to the natural relation of defense strengthening, and this monotonicity serves as a consistency condition of the definition. Theoretical vulnerability bounds are proved for a deterministic decision operator h through the decision-level robustness metric Rclass (h). For tasks with discrete output, including LLM-as-a-Judge, ASR is a consistent estimator of the vulnerability V (h) with respect to a fixed attack family 

Aeval . The population limit of the empirical estimate ̂ Remp (f ) = 1 − ASR is 1 − V (h), which is an upper bound on Rclass (h) under the stated assumptions. This justifies the use of the ASR metric in the experimental part of the work. The result was obtained by the author and is theoretically justified. 2. The robustness of LLM-as-a-Judge systems to injection attacks is formalized. Attacks are divided into two classes by the mechanism of influence: instructive attacks, which inject a direct command, and content attacks, which shift the verdict by manipulating the content. For each class, a corresponding countermeasure is defined. On this basis, the adaptive attack generation algorithm ASA ( Adaptive Search-Based Attack ) is developed: an evolutionary search that combines token-level mutations with semantic paraphrasing. On a set of open and proprietary models, ASA achieves an ASR of up to 73.8% under black-box access. Open models proved systematically more vulnerable than their proprietary counterparts. Attack transferability between open models reaches TSR = 62.6%. All these results were obtained experimentally by the author. 3. For the Pythia-1.4B model under full white-box access on the Trojan Detection Challenge 2023 data, the asymmetry of the trojan detection problem is quantitatively characterized. A surrogate trigger activates the trojan almost always: REASR ( Reverse-Engineered Attack Success Rate ) is ≈ 0.99. The original trigger cannot be reconstructed: Recall ≈ 0.17 is close to the baseline level ≈ 0.14. The gap is explained by the combinatorial growth of the space of discrete triggers and indicates the limitations of post-training trojan auditing. Priority should be given to preventive data control. The author hypothesizes that the asymmetry grows with the number of model parameters. 4. A methodology for generating and classifying multi-step prompt injection attacks on LLMs equipped with multi-layer defense systems, including system instructions, external Python filters, and LLM filters, is developed and experimentally evaluated. On the international SaTML CTF 2024 benchmark, four classes of successful bypass vectors are systematized, namely code-oriented attacks, ASCII character encoding, word-splitting attacks, and contextual misdirection. For each class, the conditions of applicability and 6effective countermeasures are determined: input normalization and context-isolated system instructions. The methodology was developed by the author and verified on the competition data. 5. A defense method for LLM-as-a-Judge systems based on adaptive committees of hetero-geneous models is developed. The method uses majority voting in the space of classified verdicts. It differs from standard ensembles in that the composition of the committee is optimized with regard to the architectural diversity of the models. The Condorcet jury theorem gives a theoretical justification for the growth of committee accuracy, that is, of the probability of a correct majority verdict, when the models err independently and each is correct with probability above 0.5. This accuracy result does not by itself establish robustness in the sense of Rclass . On a representative set of attacks, a committee of 5–7 models of different architectural families reduces the ASR by 47–55 percentage points for the most vulnerable open-weight model (Gemma-3-4B) compared with the single model. The method was developed and experimentally validated by the author. 6. An approach to the security analysis of agentic LLM systems based on the Model Context Protocol is proposed. The class of prompt injection to tool hijacking attacks is formalized. Architectural vulnerabilities of MCP are identified, and a set of defense mechanisms is developed. Its core consists of two algorithms. AttestMCP restricts tool calls to a session capability table and controls packet integrity with an HMAC signature at an overhead below 0.1 ms per call. Commit Boundary runs the code generated by the agent in an isolated environment. They are complemented by four auxiliary mechanisms: capability certificates, provenance tagging, enforced isolation of cross-server transfer, and replay protection. All components are combined in the MCPSec software module, which reduces attack success while preserving an acceptable response time. The approach was proposed by the author and confirmed experimentally on the MCPBench benchmark. 

Theoretical and practical significance. The theoretical significance of the work lies in the creation of a formal framework for evaluating LLM robustness. The metric Rstab (f ) is monotone with respect to defense strengthening, and the related decision-level metric Rclass (h)gives a proved upper bound V (h) ≤ 1 − Rclass (h) on the vulnerability under localized attacks. Together they provide a quantitative basis for comparing defense methods. The Condorcet jury theorem justifies the growth of the accuracy of majority-vote committees under independent errors and a base accuracy above 0.5. For compositional systems, a Lipschitz decomposition of sensitivity is obtained. Its empirical plug-in estimates indicate which component of the pipeline dominates the sensitivity. The discovered asymmetry of trojan detection favors preventive data auditing over post-training diagnostics. The practical significance of the work lies in two registered software suites and a module for agentic LLM systems. JudgeGuard (software registration certificate No. 2025687702) automates the testing of LLM-as-a-Judge and implements committee-based defense. TrojanArmor (certificate No. 2025684247) detects trojans. The MCPSec software module controls the permissions of tool calls and isolates code execution in agentic LLM systems. The areas of application are the security auditing of automatic evaluation systems, dialogue agents, and agentic platforms. The results of the thesis have been adopted in industry. In the production activity of Viasat, as confirmed by an implementation certificate, the framework for evaluating LLM-as-a-Judge vulnerabilities, the defense method based on model committees, and the methodology of multi-step attacks were applied to secure a recommendation ranking module built on the LLM-as-a-Judge architecture. 

Research methods. The work combines theoretical and experimental methods. The scientific literature on attacks on LLMs, including prompt injections and trojans, and on methods of defense and robustness evaluation was analyzed. Models of attacks and defenses were developed and formalized. The approaches were validated through participation in international LLM 7security competitions: Trojan Detection Challenge 2023, SaTML CTF 2024, and Kaggle “LLMs: You Can’t Please Them All”. The empirical part included the deployment and configuration of open and commercial LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama 2, Llama 3.2, Gemma, Claude-3-Opus). Both the attacks developed by the author and attacks known from the literature were reproduced and simulated: CUA (Comparative Undermining Attack ), JMA ( Justification Manipulation Attack ), ASA ( Adaptive Search-Based Attack ), code-oriented injections, and others. The effectiveness of defense scenarios was evaluated by statistical methods: bootstrap analysis, hypothesis testing, and confidence intervals. Adversarial examples were generated by two algorithms: the author’s ASA and the Greedy Coordinate Gradient method (GCG). 

Main statements submitted for defense: 

1. A formal mathematical model for evaluating the robustness of large language models (Rstab (f )). The model relies on a divergence measure between probability distributions in the discrete token space and serves as a metric of generative robustness. For the class of localized attacks, the upper bound V (h) ≤ 1 − Rclass (h) is proved. For non-localized attacks, such as prompt injection and jailbreak, an empirical calibrated model 

V (h) ≈ g(1 − Rclass (h)) is proposed. The upper bound is rigorously proved by the author. The empirical model is a hypothesis whose parameters are calibrated on a validation set of attacks. 2. The adaptive adversarial attack generation algorithm (ASA), which implements an evolu-tionary search with selection by an objective function that measures the influence on the judge model. The algorithm combines token-level mutations with semantic paraphrasing. In the black-box setting, the ASR reaches 73.8%. Reverse engineering of trojans revealed a practical asymmetry: generating adversarial inputs is substantially easier than detecting them (REASR ≈ 0.99 with Recall ≈ 0.17). This gap argues for prioritizing preventive defense. The ASA algorithm and the asymmetry analysis are the work of the author. 3. A defense method for LLM-as-a-Judge systems based on adaptive committees of heteroge-neous models, which reduces the ASR by 47–55 percentage points for the most vulnerable model Gemma-3-4B, namely from 73.8% to 19.3% with a committee of 7 models. The theoretical justification through the Condorcet jury theorem concerns committee accuracy and holds under independent errors and the condition r > 0.5, where r denotes the base ac-curacy of an individual model. For Gemma-3-4B, whose base accuracy does not satisfy this condition, the gain comes primarily from the heterogeneous composition of the committee, which decorrelates the vulnerabilities of the base models. Multi-layer defense (defense in depth) reduces the ASR from 90% to 15–25%. Four classes of bypasses of multi-layer filters are systematized. The results were obtained experimentally. 4. An architectural and algorithmic approach to defending agentic LLM systems based on MCP. It includes the MCPBench benchmark (847 scenarios), the AttestMCP algorithm for attestation of tool calls, the Commit Boundary software isolation pattern, and four auxiliary defense mechanisms: capability certificates, provenance tagging, enforced isolation, and replay protection. On the MCPBench benchmark, the approach reduces the average ASR of agentic attacks from 53.7% to 12.4%. The approach was developed and validated by the author. 5. The proposed methods for evaluating and improving the robustness of LLM systems can be implemented as a complete software toolkit with measurable performance. This is confirmed by the JudgeGuard (certificate No. 2025687702) and TrojanArmor (certificate No. 2025684247) software suites, as well as by the MCPSec module. TrojanArmor was validated on the TDC2023 benchmark, JudgeGuard on SaTML CTF 2024 and deployed in the production environment of Viasat, and MCPSec on the MCPBench benchmark. 8Reliability. The reliability of the results rests on the following. The experimental base is built on the public datasets MT-Bench, TDC2023, SaTML CTF 2024, and Kaggle “LLMs: You Can’t Please Them All”. The models represent different architectural families: Gemma, Llama, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3-Opus. Each model/attack/task combination was tested on

# Analysis of Threats and Vulnerabilities of Large Language Models 

# Chapter Overview 

The security of large language models is determined by the interaction of many factors: the discrete nature of the input space, the diversity of attack vectors, and the architectural features of LLM-based systems. This chapter formalizes the threat space, classifies vulnerabilities, and analyzes the specifics of attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge architectures and on systems with multi-layer defense. The results form the theoretical basis for the defense methods developed in the subsequent chapters. 

# 1.1 Theoretical Foundations of Large Language Model Robust-ness 

Terminological convention. This work uses the following terminology. Robustness denotes the ability of a model to preserve correct behavior under perturbations of the input data. The term covers the notions of adversarial robustness and certified robustness used in the literature. The subscript “stab” in Rstab (f ) comes from the word stability : the metric evaluates how much the output distributions of the model change under input perturbations. Rstab (f ) is a robustness metric, and no separate notion of stability is introduced. The terms trojan and backdoor are used as synonyms. In the LLM-as-a-Judge architecture, an LLM performs automatic evaluation. This evaluating model is called the judge model , or simply the judge .Below we give definitions and formalisms for the analysis of LLM robustness and security, the types of vulnerabilities, and approaches to their quantitative evaluation. 

1.1.1 Large Language Models: A Formal Representation 

Large language models (LLMs) are a class of probabilistic sequence models. As a rule, they are built on deep neural networks with the Transformer architecture [18] and trained on large corpora of text data [19]. Such models are intended for processing and generating natural language text. They operate on sequences of discrete units, tokens. A formalization of LLMs and of their inputs and outputs is necessary for a rigorous definition and analysis of model properties, including robustness. 

Definition 1.1. Token and sequence space. Let V denote a finite vocabulary of tokens. The space of all finite token sequences, that is, texts, is defined as X = V∗, where V∗ is the Kleene 11 closure of the set V. The length of an arbitrary sequence x ∈ X is written as |x|.The input of the model is a token sequence x ∈ X . The nature of the output depends on the task. In general form, an LLM is given by a parametric mapping fθ with a parameter vector θ:

fθ : X → P (Y),

where Y is the space of possible model outputs, and P(Y) is the set of probability distributions on it. • In text generation tasks, such as autocompletion, translation, and dialogue, the model predicts a probability distribution over the vocabulary V for the next token or generates an entire token sequence. In this case Y often coincides with X , that is, the space of output sequences, or with V, that is, the space of next tokens, and fθ defines the conditional probability distribution pθ(y|x) for y ∈ Y given the input x ∈ X .• In text classification tasks, such as sentiment analysis and topic detection, Y is a finite set of class labels, and fθ(x) defines a probability distribution over these labels. The discrete nature of the input space X fundamentally distinguishes LLMs from models that operate on continuous data, in particular in computer vision [20], and imposes constraints on the methods for evaluating and improving robustness. For brevity, the subscript θ is omitted below, and the model is denoted as f , which implies a fixed set of parameters. When an output predicate is applied to f (x), as in f (x) ∈ Y bad , the symbol f (x) denotes a response y ∼ p(·| x) generated by the model, and the probability is taken over this generation. Chapter 2 distinguishes stochastic generation from deterministic decoding h = Dec ◦ f .

1.1.2 LLM Robustness: Definition and Significance 

The robustness of a large language model is defined as its ability to preserve correct behavior under small, semantically insignificant, or syntactically admissible perturbations of the input data. The reliability of dialogue systems, automated content creation, and decision support systems directly depends on this property [1, 21]. With insufficient robustness, even a minimal variation of a query can lead to the generation of erroneous or undesirable responses [22]. 1.1.3 LLM Vulnerabilities and Attack Types 

Insufficient robustness of LLMs opens opportunities for attacks on model behavior. The goals of an adversary range from generating toxic or prohibited content to extracting confidential information from the training data, bypassing safety systems, and disrupting the normal operation of the model [5, 6, 25]. Vulnerability is formalized through the probability that the model exhibits undesirable behavior under the worst-case perturbation within a given budget. The local vulnerability metric Vlocal (f ) reflects the expected maximum probability of obtaining undesirable outputs, which form the set Ybad ⊆ Y defined formally in Section 2.2.3 of Chapter 2, under perturbations of a specific input query within its ϵ-neighborhood. In essence, it quantifies how easily the model can be induced to generate prohibited or harmful content by modifying the query. The rigorous definition and the mathematical properties of the metric Vlocal (f ), and its relation to Rstab (f ) and to the global vulnerability index V (f ), are considered in Chapter 2. The main types of attacks on LLMs are described below. 1. Adversarial attacks: The construction of perturbed inputs x′ that differ from x only slightly, often imperceptibly to a human, that is, such that d(x, x ′) is small under some metric 

d, in particular dL or dS . Such perturbations lead to a model output f (x′) that is erroneous in classification tasks or undesirable in generation tasks [1, 3, 24]. For LLMs, this can be the substitution of characters and tokens, the insertion or deletion of words, paraphrasing, or the use of control sequences [3, 4, 26]. Gradient optimization methods, including Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), are used to generate such attacks. 2. Prompt injection attacks: The injection of instructions into a user query, often covert or disguised, with the goal of overriding the original system instructions of the model or its safety mechanisms [5, 27]. A typical example is the addition of text such as “Ignore the previous instructions and do X”. For such attacks, the perturbation need not be small under the formal metrics d, because they exploit the ability of LLMs to follow instructions [8, 27]. Two targets are particularly exposed: LLM-as-a-Judge systems, where injections distort the evaluation results, and LLMs with multi-layer defense, which specialized techniques can bypass. 3. Jailbreaking attacks: The design of specially crafted prompts that force the model to generate content that violates its internal safety policies: instructions for illegal actions, malicious code, offensive material. The safety mechanisms established by alignment fail to activate [25, 28]. Both large commercial and open models are vulnerable to such attacks [29–31], including under multi-turn queries or techniques that exploit the features of multi-layer defense systems. 4. Backdoor attacks: An adversary with access to the training process implants hidden triggers into the model at the training or fine-tuning stage [32]. A trigger can be a phrase or a symbol whose presence in the input data x′ causes behavior predefined by the attacker: the generation of a specific text or an incorrect classification [33, 34]. Detecting trojans (backdoors) is difficult, because the model behaves normally on ordinary data. Reverse engineering of triggers, that is, the reconstruction of the original trigger from a known malicious output, remains a task with low detection recall even under controlled conditions. 14 5. Data extraction attacks: Attempts to reconstruct or extract from the model confidential information that could have been present in its training data, including personal data and proprietary texts, by submitting specially constructed queries [6]. 6. Attacks on agentic systems through tool hijacking: With the development of agentic LLM systems that interact with external tools through standardized protocols, in particular the Model Context Protocol (MCP) [35], a class of attacks arises in which a prompt injection into the context of a tool leads to an unauthorized tool call with arbitrary arguments. Unlike classical prompt injection, the consequences of such attacks go beyond text generation and include code execution, file system modification, and network requests [10, 11]. The criticality of such attacks is amplified by architectural features of agentic interaction protocols, namely the absence of capability attestation, unauthenticated sampling, and implicit trust propagation between servers. A detailed study of this issue is given in Chapter 5. The robustness metric Rstab (f ) and the local vulnerability metric Vlocal (f ) capture different aspects of model behavior. The first evaluates how much the distribution p(y|x) changes under input perturbations, and the second characterizes the probability of generating y ∈ Y bad . There is, however, a conceptual connection between them. A model with high Rstab (f ), that is, with small changes of p(y|x) under perturbations x′, is presumably less vulnerable and shows a low 

Vlocal (f ). If the distribution p(y|x) hardly changes under the transition to x′, then the probability of generating undesirable content P (y ∈ Y bad |x′) also changes insignificantly. In the general case, this connection is not a strict mathematical dependence. By Pinsker’s inequality, a change of the distribution with a small Kullback–Leibler divergence DKL changes the probability of falling into Ybad by at most √DKL /2 in absolute terms. In relative terms, however, this probability can grow by orders of magnitude if the original probability was close to zero. Conversely, a large 

DKL does not necessarily lead to the generation of undesirable content when the new distribution is concentrated on admissible outputs. The results of experiments with adversarial training [2] indicate a correlation between an increase of Rstab (f ) and a decrease of Vlocal (f ). No rigorous theoretical guarantees of such a dependence, however, have been obtained.

Of the threats classified above, this thesis analyzes prompt injection, jailbreak attacks, and trojans further, both formally and experimentally. Attacks on agentic LLM systems that interact with external tools form a separate class. In the OWASP classification, they relate to excessive agency (LLM08) and insecure plugins (LLM07). Chapter 5 considers them in detail for MCP. 

1.2.3 Threat Categorization 

The threats described above are grouped by six security aspects (Fig. 1.2). 

Data and model integrity. Training data poisoning (LLM03 in OWASP), backdoor attacks, and adversarial examples distort the parameters or the behavior of the model. The local vulnerability Vlocal (fθ) partially reflects this aspect when incorrect responses are included in Ybad .

Confidentiality is affected by a spectrum of threats: model inversion, membership inference, training data extraction, and sensitive information disclosure, all of which correspond to LLM06, as well as system prompt disclosure [67]. The quantitative evaluation relies on the metric Pleak 

and on the mutual information I(fθ(X); C). The availability aspect is represented by denial of service (LLM04), which disrupts the operation of the service for legitimate users. The governance and control aspect covers the widest list of threats: prompt injection (LLM01), insecure output handling (LLM02), supply chain vulnerabilities (LLM05), insecure plugin design (LLM07), excessive agency (LLM08), model theft (LLM10), jailbreaking, and detection evasion. Minimizing E(p) for plugins directly reduces the risks of this category. 

Ethics and fairness are affected by bias amplification and by the generation of socially unacceptable content through jailbreaking, prompt injection, and adversarial examples. The metrics Abias and Vlocal (fθ), with an appropriate definition of Ybad , can be used for the evaluation. 

Reliability and reproducibility depend on the non-determinism of generation, which affects the predictability of behavior and is captured by the metric N Rep (fθ, x ). Some threats affect several categories at once: prompt injection can lead to data leakage, integrity violation, and the generation of malicious content.

1.2.5 Illustrative Threat Examples 

Three examples illustrate the threat classes considered above with the results of controlled experiments and LLM security competitions. 

1. Vulnerability of LLM-as-a-Judge to targeted prompt injection attacks. 

22 • Description: LLM-as-a-Judge architectures are susceptible to prompt injection attacks. The Comparative Undermining Attack (CUA) changes the decision of the judge directly. The Justification Manipulation Attack (JMA) distorts the justification of the verdict. Both attacks are implemented with optimization methods, in particular Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG). • Effectiveness: On the Gemma, Llama, GPT-4, and Claude-3-Opus models with the MT-Bench dataset and Kaggle tasks, CUA and the Adaptive Search-Based Attack (ASA), defined in Chapter 3, achieved a high success rate, especially on open-weight models. Quantitative results are given in Chapter 4 (Table 4.1). 

2. Difficulties of detecting trojans in LLMs. The Trojan Detection Challenge 2023 studied the reverse engineering of trojans in the Pythia model. The reconstruction of the true triggers proved to be a fundamentally hard task, whereas the generation of surrogate triggers that cause the desired output was achieved much more easily. These results indicate the priority of preventive measures. A detailed analysis of the results is given in Chapter 4 (Section 4.2.2). 

3. Bypassing a multi-layer defense with prompt injection. The SaTML CTF 2024 competition targeted attacks on LLMs, in particular GPT-3.5 and Llama 2, equipped with a three-layer defense: system instructions, a Python filter, and an LLM filter. Participants developed and applied various prompt injection techniques, including multi-turn attacks and specific wordings for bypassing filters. The multi-layer defense configurations could be bypassed. The success of an attack was determined by the sophistication of the prompt and the specifics of the filters. Defense strategies need constant adaptation and supplementation with dynamic detection methods. 

1.2.6 Analysis of Threat Interrelations 

The threat types for LLMs are interrelated: countering one threat affects the vulnerability to others. Rigorous theorems valid for all LLMs are difficult to formulate, so below we give well-founded assumptions and principles supported by the literature. 

Principle 1: Relation between data extraction and sensitive information disclosure. 

Training data extraction (10) is a special case of sensitive information disclosure (11). Data extraction attacks aim to reconstruct fragments of Dtrain [6], whereas leakage can also occur passively [69]. Assumption: The success of targeted data extraction attacks Sextract , defined in Section 1.2.2, serves as a lower bound for the more general risk of confidential information leakage 

Pleak under comparable definitions of the metrics. Defense measures against memorization, in particular differential privacy methods [70, 71], reduce the risks of both threats [6]. 

Principle 2: Relation between jailbreaking, injections, and detection evasion. 

Prompt injection techniques (1) often serve as a means to implement jailbreaking attacks (14) aimed at bypassing the built-in defense mechanisms of the model [25]. At the same time, a successful jailbreaking attack does not necessarily mean an evasion of the detection system (17): an external detector D can classify the malicious query xjb as dangerous. The difficulty of creating effective jailbreaking queries and the difficulty of detecting them are related, but advanced attacks are designed to bypass both the internal mechanisms of the model and external detectors simultaneously [3]. Countering them therefore requires both an improvement of robust alignment [72] and the refinement of external detectors [73]. 

Principle 3: Effect of reproducibility on security. The dependence between LLM non-determinism (15) and security is not monotonic [74]. On the one hand, the stochasticity of generation hinders the creation of precise adversarial examples (6) and jailbreaking attacks (14) that require a strictly defined response [75]. On the other hand, it also complicates the testing and verification of defense mechanisms. Rare but dangerous vulnerabilities manifest stochastically and are therefore harder to detect. The reliability of attack detectors (17) also 23 drops when the reproducibility of system behavior is low. The choice of the optimal level of determinism is dictated by the specific application and the threat profile. 

Principle 4: Relation between bias and confidentiality. Bias amplification (12) and sensitive information disclosure (11) are related through a common mechanism: the memorization of correlations in the training data. A model that has learned associations between sensitive demographic attributes and other data in Dtrain simultaneously amplifies biases and becomes vulnerable to membership inference attacks (5) for individuals from the corresponding groups [76, 77]. The dependence is bidirectional. Weakening the association of the model with sensitive attributes reduces bias and increases confidentiality. Conversely, strict confidentiality measures, in particular DP [70], reduce accuracy disproportionately on underrepresented subgroups and can thereby amplify bias [78]. 

Principle 5: Cascading risks of the supply chain and plugins. Vulnerabilities in the supply chain (13) directly affect the security of the integrated components, including plugins (16). The compromise of a base model or library entails the compromise of all dependent plugins. An insecure plugin with excessive privileges, that is, with a high value of E(p) from Section 1.2.2, creates an attack vector against the base model or other system components. The risks of these two categories compound each other, which requires a systematic approach: privilege control, formalized procedures for integrating third-party extensions, and regular audits of the components in use [45]. 

Principle 6: Fundamental trade-off. Many aspects of LLM security and functionality trade off against each other. For example: • Utility vs. safety/censorship: Overly strict defense mechanisms aimed against jailbreaking and toxicity can reduce the utility of the model and its ability to fulfill legitimate requests, which is known as the “lobotomy effect” [25]. • Confidentiality vs. utility: Confidentiality methods, for example DP, often reduce the accuracy and overall performance of the model [71, 78]. • Robustness vs. accuracy: Methods for improving robustness to adversarial attacks, for exam-ple adversarial training, can reduce accuracy on clean data, in some settings substantially [2, 79]. In practice, these trade-offs limit the simultaneous maximization of all target properties of an LLM system. The specific application scenario determines the prioritization among security, utility, confidentiality, and computational efficiency. LLM security does not reduce to countering isolated threats: defense measures must account for the interaction between threats and for the system properties of the model in the context of its deployment. 

1.2.7 Conclusions and Risk Mitigation Recommendations 

The threats affect all stages of working with a model, from data preparation to deployment. Countering them requires a combination of technical, organizational, and methodological mea-sures. • Preventive measures at the development and training stage. Auditing and filtering of the training data reduce the risks of poisoning (LLM03, 2, 3) [80] and bias (12) [81]. Privacy-enhancing methods, such as differentially private training with DP-SGD [71] and federated learning [82], counter data leakage (LLM06, 4, 5, 10, 11) [70]. Adversarial training [2] and safety alignment [36, 83] increase robustness to adversarial attacks (6) and jailbreaking (14). Control of the provenance and integrity of supply chain components (LLM05, 13) is also a preventive measure [84]. 24 • Defense mechanisms at the operation stage. At the input level, query validation and sanitization are applied to prevent injections (LLM01) [27, 85], together with the separation of data and instructions [86]. At the output level, filtering and sanitization are performed before use in other systems (LLM02, 9) [58]. Access control and authentication of the model API protect against theft (LLM10) [57]. Resource monitoring and limits counter DoS (LLM04) [87], and the principle of least privilege for plugins counters the LLM07 threat [88]. The detection of anomalous behavior and of attempts to bypass the defense (17) complements these measures [3, 25]. • Continuous monitoring and risk management. Red teaming and penetration testing [89, 90] reveal previously unknown attack vectors. Event log analysis and action auditing [91] ensure prompt incident detection. The model and the defense mechanisms must be updated regularly in response to changes in the threat landscape. Quantitative measurement of the security level with the metrics Rstab (fθ) and Vlocal (fθ) makes it possible to track the dynamics [21]. Formal risk assessment methods combined with the control measures of the NIST and OWASP standards make it possible to build a multi-layer defense system for LLMs under real operating conditions. A systematic analysis of the vulnerabilities of agentic coding systems, covering 42 attack techniques and 18 defense mechanisms, is presented in [10]. The subsequent parts of the thesis are devoted to the experimental validation of specific methods for countering the threats considered here. 

1.2.8 Formalization of the Threat Space for Large Language Models 

The systematization of threats rests on a formal structure that describes the components and their interrelations. Abstracting from specific attack implementations makes it possible to reveal general patterns. 

Definition 1.3. LLM threat space. The threat space for a large language model fθ : X → P(Y), in the notation of Section 1.2, is defined as the tuple T = ( Atype , V, IAV , CV V ), where: • Atype is the set of attack types , for example Prompt Injection, Data Poisoning, CUA, JMA, and others, corresponding to the classes from Section 1.2.2. The superscript type emphasizes that this is a classification of attacks. In Chapter 2, the symbol A denotes a family of attacks as functions. • V is the set of specific vulnerabilities of the model or of the system in which it operates, for example: v1 = “insufficient sanitization of user input in the system prompt”, v2 = “presence of memorized PII in the model parameters”, v3 = “vulnerability of LLM-as-a-Judge to justification manipulation”. • IAV ⊆ A type × V is the relation “attack exploits vulnerability”. Principle 1.1. On the prioritization of vulnerability remediation 

Let G = ( V, CV V ) be the vulnerability dependency graph, where V is the set of vulnerabilities, and CV V is the dependency relation. The application of defense measures aimed at remediating vulnerabilities must account for the structure of this graph. 1. Priority to root vulnerabilities: In an acyclic dependency graph, vulnerabilities without incoming edges, that is, root vulnerabilities, are the root causes of other vulnerabilities. Their remediation has a cascading effect: it prevents the exploitation of each dependent vulnerability that arises or becomes exploitable only through this root. A dependent vulnerability that is also reachable from another root, or that is present independently, requires its own remediation. 2. Breaking dependency cycles: In a graph that contains cycles, priority should be given to remediating vulnerabilities in the strongly connected components that are sources, that is, have no incoming edges, in the condensation graph. Remediating even one vulnerability in a cycle breaks it, which can significantly reduce the overall risk. The principle forms a methodological basis for planning defense measures. It directs resources to the remediation of the vulnerabilities that are most critical from the standpoint of structural dependencies. Accounting for the dependencies CV V when planning defense measures relies on algorithms for finding strongly connected components and for topological sorting from the standard toolkit of graph theory [93, 94]. Their application to the LLM vulnerability dependency graph provides a tool for prioritizing security measures. Research on vulnerabilities and the development of attacks on large language models carry dual-use risks. All experiments were conducted in a controlled environment and were aimed at identifying vulnerabilities, quantitatively assessing risks, and developing defense mechanisms. 

Legal and ethical scope of the tests. When interacting with commercial APIs, in particular GPT-4 and Claude-3, their terms of use were strictly observed. No attacks were performed that aimed at disrupting services, at unauthorized access to the data of other users, or at the generation and dissemination of dangerous content outside the research environment. The goal of all attacks was to evaluate the vulnerabilities of the models for the subsequent development of defense mechanisms. 

Data handling. No real personal or confidential data were used in the experiments. Datasets such as MT-Bench are publicly available and intended for research purposes. When generating prompts and analyzing responses, measures were taken to prevent the accidental processing of sensitive information. 

Generation safety. In jailbreaking attacks or attacks aimed at generating malicious content, filtering mechanisms were applied to prevent the creation and storage of dangerous material. The results of such attacks were analyzed in terms of structure and mechanics, not content. 

Responsible disclosure. The research results are published to inform the scientific commu-nity and developers with the goal of improving the defense of systems. Ready-made attack tools that could be used by malicious actors are not published. The dual-use nature of work in AI security requires adherence to ethical norms at every stage of the research.

# Theoretical Foundations for Evaluating the Robustness of Large Language Models 

# Chapter Overview 

Quantifying LLM robustness requires a mathematical framework that accounts for the discrete token space and the characteristics of adversarial attacks. This chapter defines robustness metrics, establishes their properties, and proves the relations between them. Subsequent chapters use this framework to develop experimental methods and defense mechanisms. 

# 2.1 Problem Statement and Threat Model 

To formalize the problem of evaluating the robustness of large language models, one must specify the input space, the output space, the family of admissible attacks, and the sets of goals and consequences of adversarial actions. We next formalize these components and state the general robustness evaluation problem. 

2.1.1 Formalization of the Robustness Evaluation Problem 

The spaces X , Y, the model fθ : X → P (Y) and the perturbation neighborhood Bϵ(x), written

In addition, we introduce the following: • A is the family of attacks, where each attack a ∈ A is given by a function a : X → X that transforms a legitimate input into a potentially malicious one. The family covers both general attack types and attacks specific to particular architectures. • G is the set of adversary goals, and R is the set of consequences of a successful attack.

2.1.2 Assumptions About the Adversary 

Robustness evaluation requires explicit assumptions about the capabilities and limitations of the adversary. The adopted classification distinguishes the following levels of knowledge: • Black-box: Access is limited to the model interface: the adversary can send queries and receive responses without any information about the internal structure, parameters, or training data. • Gray-box: Partial information about the model is known, for example the architecture, but the specific parameter values are not available. • White-box: Full access to the model is assumed, including the architecture, parameters, and training data. The GCG method, in particular, requires this level of access to compute gradients. Along with the level of knowledge, the following constraints matter: • Query budget: The maximum number of queries that the adversary can send to the model. • Computational constraints: The available computational resources, which limit the complexity of the algorithms used. • Time constraints: The admissible duration of the attack. These assumptions set the boundaries of the attack space A and directly affect the outcome of robustness evaluation. 30 2.1.3 Classification of Adversarial Actions 

Adversarial actions against large language models can be classified into four main categories: • Semantically equivalent reformulations: The wording of the query changes while the semantic content is preserved. Paraphrasing with synonyms or restructuring the sentence are typical examples of such an action. • Malicious context injections: Specially crafted instructions or context aimed at changing the behavior of the model are added to the query. This category includes prompt injection, jailbreak attacks, and other forms of context manipulation. Specific variants include the Comparative Undermining Attack (CUA) and Justification Manipulation Attack (JMA), which target LLM-as-a-Judge systems, as well as code-based attacks and the Distraction attack against defended systems. • Tokenization manipulations: Features of the tokenization process are exploited to bypass defense mechanisms. In particular, special characters split forbidden words into fragments, as in the Word-splitting attack, and none of the individual fragments is detected by the filters. • Denial-of-service attacks: The actions aim to degrade model performance or to stop the service completely. Queries that cause excessive resource consumption or looping are a characteristic example. Each type of action requires dedicated robustness evaluation methods and corresponding defense measures.

Remark 2.24. On the stochasticity of LLMs. The lemma assumes deterministic models, corresponding to greedy decoding with deterministic argmax selection at each generation step. In general, LLMs are stochastic. Extending the lemma to the stochastic case requires a definition of robustness for distributions, as in Rstab , and an account of how stochasticity propagates through the composition, which is a harder problem. In the deterministic case, the inequality Rclass (h) ≥ Rclass (g) is global: the second component 

f can only “merge” distinct outputs of g, but cannot “split” coinciding ones. The robustness of the composition does not decrease. At the same time, the inequality is a worst-case bound and assumes that both components are deterministic, with a fixed direction of composition h = f ◦ g.Arbitrary addition of components to the system does not guarantee that robustness is preserved: the reverse order of composition, stochastic decoding, or feedback loops between components make the bound inapplicable. For stochastic models, an analogous statement requires a separate justification, because noise in f can break robustness even when g is robust. The relation of this result to the bound on Vd for recursive chains is discussed in Remark 2.34.

2.5.2 Recursive Chains of LLM Agents 

In recursive chains of LLM agents, one agent’s output becomes another’s input, creating a risk that errors accumulate and become amplified.

# 2.7 Chapter Conclusions 

The problem of evaluating LLM robustness is formalized through the definition of the input space, the output space, the attack family, and the sets of goals and consequences. The chapter distinguishes adversarial robustness AdvRobustness( f ) from robustness to small input perturbations, measured by Rstab (f ). Formal metrics are proposed: Rclass (h) for classification tasks, Rstab (f ) for generative models, V (f ) for global vulnerability, and Risk( a, f ) for quantifying 47 the risk of a specific attack. For practical application, the normalization parameter Cmax and the choice of the divergence measure D are specified. Analytical upper bounds for Rclass (h) are obtained, and the consistency of empirical estimates is confirmed as a consequence of the law of large numbers for bounded random variables. 4. Representation-Level Methods. A number of approaches aim to increase the robustness of the intermediate and input representations of the model. Adaptive normalization, in particular Conditional Layer Normalization and feature-wise conditioning [109], allows the model to adjust the normalization parameters to a specific input, which reduces sensitivity to anomalous requests. Contrastive learning at the level of hidden states, in which semantically similar inputs are mapped to nearby points of the representation space, increases semantic robustness. For this purpose, InfoNCE loss functions [113] are applied to intermediate Transformer layers [114, 115]. Architectural modifications usually require retraining the model and can affect its performance and computational cost. 

3.2.2 Multi-Layer Defense with a Focus on Robustness 

The principle of defense in depth, whose practical deployment is considered in Chapter 6 (Section 6.1.1), also applies to improving the overall robustness of a system. It relies on the formalization of LLM security as a service from Chapter 2 (Section 2.1.4): each stage of the request-processing pipeline becomes a point where defense mechanisms are applied, which reduces the sensitivity of the system to unforeseen changes at the input or output. Specific implementations, including system instructions, Python filters, and LLM filters, are studied in [13] together with their vulnerability to targeted attacks. Perplexity Check, Instruction Filtering, and Content Moderation complement this set. The defense is organized in three layers (Fig. 3.1). Before a request reaches the model, the input layer normalizes the text: it removes extraneous characters, normalizes case, and corrects obvious typos. Anomaly detectors (Perplexity Check, Instruction Filtering) and Python filters reject requests with characteristic attack patterns, and smoothing transformations [116] are applied to the input. The architectural solutions of Section 3.2.1, the training methods of Section 3.3, and the modification of system instructions form the model layer . In addition, regularization of internal states [117] penalizes sharp changes of activations under small modifications of the input. At the output layer , probability calibration through temperature scaling [118] and response ensembling [107] increase reliability. The generated text is checked for consistency and for the absence of confidential information, after which LLM filters and Python filters complete the sanitization. The sequence of checks of the three layers is organized as a pipeline with early rejection.

Adversarial training effectively strengthens robustness to the types of attacks used during training. At the same time, it can reduce performance on clean data and require substantial computational resources. This trade-off, called the robustness-accuracy trade-off, is studied theoretically in [79, 121].

In distillation [99], a compact “student model” ( fS ) is trained to reproduce the behavior of a large “teacher model” ( fT ). 2. Adversarial Distillation [129]. Adversarial attacks are applied during distillation. The student fS is trained to be similar to the teacher fT not only on clean data but also on adversarial examples generated to attack the teacher or the student.

The best results are often achieved by combining several approaches, for example adversarial training and data augmentation. 57 3.3.3 Theoretical Limitations and Trade-offs 

Methods for improving robustness are subject to a number of fundamental limitations. The most studied of them is the robustness-accuracy trade-off. Theoretical analysis [79, 121] and empirical data [2] show that robust models, being less sensitive to input variations, capture fewer of the fine distinctions in the data that are necessary for maximal accuracy. A second trade-off concerns computational cost: generating adversarial examples at every training step multiplies the cost of training. The generalization of robustness is limited. A model that is robust to one type of perturbation, for example to token substitutions within dL ≤ ϵ, can remain vulnerable to semantic paraphrases or character-level attacks [22]. Moreover, defense against one class of attacks sometimes increases vulnerability to others [131]. The transferability of attacks between architectures is also nonuni-form: attacks that are effective against open-weight models may not transfer to proprietary systems. Theoretical results [79] indicate that perfect robustness to all admissible perturbations cannot be achieved while preserving high accuracy. The evaluation of robustness also depends substantially on the choice of the metric d and the budget ϵ: robustness achieved for small ϵ may not hold for large ones, which makes the choice of realistic parameters decisive [132].

Differences between ASA and classical genetic algorithms. Although ASA is based on the evolutionary paradigm, it differs fundamentally from the standard genetic algorithms (GA) used to generate adversarial examples in the domain of images and continuous data. The first difference concerns the mutation operators. ASA operates with synonym substitu-tion, semantics-preserving paraphrasing, and insertion of distracting fragments. Grammatical correctness of the candidates is essential for bypassing linguistic filters, and the bitwise or real-valued mutations of standard GAs do not preserve it. The second difference concerns the objective function. In ASA it has two components: Fitness( xi) = α · ASR( xi) + β · Stealth( xi). Here ASR( xi) ∈ [0 , 1] expresses the success of the attack, and Stealth( xi) = max(0 , sim( xi, x orig )) ∈ [0 , 1] reflects the cosine similarity of the embeddings of the attack prompt and the original prompt, truncated from below at zero to map it to the interval [0 , 1]. The weights α, β > 0 set the balance between effectiveness and stealth. Classical GAs for adversarial examples optimize only the distance to the decision boundary. Finally, ASA accounts for the specifics of the LLM-as-a-Judge interface. The attack aims to change the verdict of the judge model, and crossover and mutation operate on structural elements of the prompt, such as the instruction, the context, and the request, rather than on arbitrary positions in the string.

In the experiments of Chapter 4, model committees, which vote over decoded verdicts, are the most effective defense. For ensembles with probability averaging, Lemma 3.4 establishes that, for jointly convex divergence measures, the sensitivity of an ensemble does not exceed the average sensitivity of its members. These measures include DT V , DKL , and the Jensen–Shannon divergence as an f -divergence (Remark 3.5). For DT , the bound requires step-wise averaging and a common reference trajectory. The lemma does not cover voting over verdicts. Adversarial training and regularization complement ensembles at the training level: the former targets deliberate attacks more precisely, while the latter is computationally cheaper. The algorithms of the model committee pipeline and of the ASA attack, with complexity estimates, are given for reproducibility. None of the described methods removes the fundamental trade-off between robustness and accuracy. A defense against one class of perturbations does not automatically transfer to other classes, and the practical applicability of each method depends on the choice of the metric d and the budget ϵ.60 Chapter 4 

# Experimental Evaluation of Robustness Enhancement Methods 

# Chapter Overview 

This chapter experimentally evaluates the vulnerability of modern LLMs to prompt injections, adversarial perturbations, and trojans. The experiments cover LLM-as-a-Judge architectures and systems with a multi-layer defense built with the methods of Chapter 3. The measurements rely on standardized and original metrics. # 4.1 Experimental Evaluation Methodology 

This section describes the methodology of the experiments: the evaluation criteria, the datasets, and the testing procedure, adapted to the discrete nature of text data. 

4.1.1 Robustness Evaluation Criteria 

To evaluate the methods for improving LLM robustness and the effectiveness of attacks, we define a set of quantitative criteria. 

Definition 4.1. Criteria for evaluating robustness and attack effectiveness. Let 

f : X → P (Y) be a language model. Remark 4.4. Relation between the theoretical metric Rstab (f ) and the experimental estimate via ASR. The theoretical robustness metric Rstab (f ), defined in Chapter 2 (Defi-nition 2.3), is computed through the step-wise divergence of the model’s internal probability distributions at each generation step. Exact computation of Rstab (f ) requires access to the full distributions. This is impossible for the proprietary models GPT-4 and Claude-3-Opus, which are available only through an API, and it is computationally expensive even for open-weight models, since it requires enumerating all x′ ∈ B ε(x). The prompt injection attacks of this chapter are not localized in Bε(x). For them, the relation rests on the calibrated empirical model of Remark 2.20, not on the theorem. 

Direction for future research: the direct computation of Rstab (f ) for the open-weight models Gemma, Llama, and Qwen by analyzing the internal distributions at each generation step, and the verification of the correlation between Rstab (f ) and the empirical proxy ̂ Remp (f ), lies beyond the scope of this work.

4.1.3 Experimental Procedure 

The experimental evaluation followed a standardized procedure that ensures the reproducibility and statistical soundness of the results. 

1. Model preparation. We define the set of models under study. It includes the open models Gemma-3, Llama-3.2, and Pythia as well as the commercial models GPT-4, Claude-3-Opus, and GPT-3.5. All models use greedy decoding with temperature=0 to ensure reproducibility. The open models run through the Hugging Face Transformers library, and the commercial models are accessed through their respective APIs. 

2. Preparation of datasets and attack/defense scenarios. The datasets described in Section 4.1.2 are selected and prepared. Attack methods are developed or adapted: Basic Injection (BI), Complex Word Bombardment (CWB), Contextual Misdirection (CM), Adaptive Search-Based Attack (ASA), Comparative Undermining Attack (CUA), Justification Manipulation Attack (JMA), code-oriented attacks, distraction attacks, system prompt attacks, and word splitting attacks. The defense mechanisms are adapted in the same way: Perplexity Check, Instruction Filtering, Content Moderation, Multi-Model Committees, and the three-layer defense from the SaTML CTF, which comprises system instructions, a Python filter, and an LLM filter. 

3. Definition of evaluation metrics. A set of metrics from Section 4.1.1 relevant to the specific experiment is selected. In particular, attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge use ASR and TSR, 64 trojan detection uses Recall and REASR, and attacks on defended systems take into account the number of attempts and the success of secret extraction. 

4. Running the experiments. Each model/system is evaluated on the relevant data with the selected attacks/defenses and metrics. Each condition uses n = 50 different prompts (Remark 4.7). 

Example of the metric computation process: 

• ASR (for LLM-as-a-Judge): The attack is applied to each of the n = 50 prompts per task. An attack is considered successful if the output score of the judge model changes by 

≥ 2 points or the comparative verdict changes. ASR = (number of successes) /n .• TSR (for LLM-as-a-Judge): Computed according to the definition from the results of transferring attacks between models. • Recall and REASR (for trojans): Computed from the BLEU comparison of predicted and true triggers and from the ability of predicted triggers to elicit the target malicious outputs, also measured through BLEU. 

5. Statistical analysis of the results. The obtained metric values are processed statistically. Means, standard deviations, and confidence intervals are computed with bootstrap resampling over 1000 iterations. Where needed, tests for the statistical significance of differences between methods are performed. To compare attack success rates, we use nonparametric procedures, in particular bootstrap estimation of the difference of proportions and tests for paired binary outcomes. Interval estimation is based on bootstrap confidence intervals. 

6. Interpretation of the results. Conclusions are drawn from the statistical analysis: a comparison of the effectiveness of different attack/defense methods, the identification of statistically significant differences, an analysis of trade-offs (RUT), and the formulation of recommendations. This procedure ensures the reproducibility and statistical soundness of the evaluation of robustness enhancement methods and of the analysis of LLM vulnerabilities.

4.2.3 Attacks on LLMs with a Multi-Layer Defense 

The results of the SaTML CTF 2024 showed that LLMs, namely GPT-3.5 and Llama 2, equipped with a three-layer defense of system instructions, a Python filter, and an LLM filter, remain vulnerable. The successful bypass vectors are systematized into four classes. For each class, we established the conditions of applicability and effective countermeasures. 1. Code-oriented attacks : the request is formatted as a fragment of code or pseudocode and bypasses text filters. Countermeasure: bring the formatting to a canonical form before filtering. 2. ASCII encoding : the characters of the request are passed as numeric codes, which hinders keyword-based detection. For GPT-3.5, a request to apply a function to the secret value produced the secret as the ASCII codes [107, 100, 75, 71, 121, 113] . Countermeasure: decode numeric representations at the input normalization stage. 3. Word splitting : target words are split into substrings that the model assembles during generation. For Llama-2, a request to create a JSON with a field Sec of 6 secret characters led to a response of the form {"Sec": ["t","D","f","J","7","R"]} . Countermeasure: join substrings during normalization. 4. Contextual redirection : the model is persuaded that the request is part of an acceptable debugging or testing scenario. This class is robust to normalization, since it operates on semantics, not syntax. Countermeasure: context-isolated system instructions that strictly separate the user and system levels of the prompt. Input normalization neutralizes the first three classes, and the fourth requires context isolation. More capable models, which handle complex instructions and code better, were vulnerable to sophisticated vectors, whereas less capable models yielded to simple requests to reveal the system prompt. In a controlled experiment on the SaTML CTF 2024 data, the three-layer defense reduced the ASR from initial values of about 90% in the control scenarios for undefended configurations to 15–25%. No combination of filters blocked all four classes at once. This demonstrates both the effectiveness of a multi-layer defense and its fundamental incompleteness. Defense mechanisms need regular updates as new vectors appear. With the described methods, the author won a prize in the attack track of the SaTML CTF 2024. 68 4.2.4 Analysis of Parameter Effects and Trade-offs 

The ASA attack implements the evolutionary scheme from Section 3.4.2 of Chapter 3 with population size N = 50, number of generations G = 20, and objective function weights α = 0 .8, 

β = 0 .2. The query budget per attack is N · G = 1000 model calls. The defense mechanisms are listed in Section 4.1.3. Their parameters, including the Perplexity Check thresholds, the Instruction Filtering rules, the Content Moderation settings, and the committee composition, directly determine the effectiveness of both the attacks and the defense mechanisms. The trade-off between robustness and utility is quantified by the RUT metric: RUT = ∆Robustness ∆Utility + ∆Cost + ϵ ,

where ∆Robustness expresses the robustness improvement, ∆Utility captures the loss of utility, and ∆Cost reflects the additional computational cost. All quantities are normalized to [0 , 1]. The parameter ϵ = 10 −6 is a regularization constant. For example, for a committee of 7 models: ∆Robustness ≈ 0.55, which corresponds to an ASR reduction from 73 .8% to 19 .3%. Here ∆Cost is measured as the normalized increase in response latency. With parallel querying of the committee members, ∆Cost ≈ 0.08 and ∆Utility ≈ 0.02, which gives RUT ≈ 5.5 and indicates a high return from this configuration. The total compute, measured in model calls, still grows linearly with the number of models, about sevenfold for this committee. Committees of models provide the best protection: the ASR against ASA drops to 19 .3%. The computational overhead grows in proportion to the number of models. Corollary 2.36, based on the Condorcet jury theorem, guarantees that the accuracy of a majority-vote committee tends to one as its size grows, if the members are independent and each has accuracy r > 0.5. If r

is estimated as 1 − ASR under ASA, the proprietary models satisfy this condition: for GPT-4 with ASR = 45 .7% the base accuracy is r ≈ 0.54, and for Claude-3-Opus with ASR = 42 .9% the value is r ≈ 0.57. The open models, in contrast, do not satisfy the condition: Llama-3.2-3B with ASR = 58 .2% gives r ≈ 0.42, and Gemma-3-4B with ASR = 73 .8% gives r ≈ 0.26. The corollary therefore does not cover the observed committee results, and their explanation is empirical. The heterogeneity of the composition decorrelates the errors of models with different architectures. An attack tailored to one model transfers to the others only partially (even between open models, TSR = 50 .5–62 .6%), so a majority can restore the correct verdict even when some members have an individual r < 0.5. This mechanism is a hypothesis consistent with the data, and the corollary does not guarantee it. Instruction Filtering and Perplexity Check are cheaper, although they are inferior to committees against complex attacks. The specific choice of strategy depends on the scenario: critical evaluation systems justify the cost of large committees, while less critical applications can rely on a combination of lightweight filters.

The generative metric Rstab (f ) has a finer granularity: even when the discrete output has changed and Rclass (h) = 0, the value of Rstab (f ) can be positive if the token-level distributions remain partially consistent. This indicates that Rstab (f ) captures information about the “hidden robustness” of the distributions, which is lost in discretization. Both metric values are small for benign perturbations. This is explained by the autoregressive nature of LLMs: even a minimal change of the input with a Levenshtein distance of 1–2 affects the internal distributions, since each subsequent token depends on all previous ones. The range of values Rstab (f ) ∈ [0 .005 , 0.540] indicates a high variability of sensitivity depending on the specific prompt. 

Limitations. The computation was performed through an API in independent generation mode, not with teacher forcing. A rigorous computation of Rstab (f ) according to Definition 2.3 requires access to the model weights. The top-20 approximation of logprobs also introduces error: the tail probability mass is underestimated, which leads to an underestimated divergence and hence an overestimated ̂ Rstab . The sample contains 20 prompts with benign perturbations. The standard deviation of Rstab across prompts, equal to 0.139, exceeds the mean of 0.115, which indicates a high variability between prompts. The results do not transfer directly to adversarial scenarios. With all these limitations, the obtained data do not contradict the hypothesis Rstab (f ) ≥ Rclass (h), but a statistically significant confirmation requires a larger sample in future studies. 

# Chapter Conclusions 

The experiments revealed consistent patterns. The adaptive ASA attack reaches ASR = 73 .8% on Gemma-3-4B, and the transferability between open models is TSR = 50 .5–62 .6%. With such 70 transferability, a defense strategy that relies on hiding the architecture is not justified. GPT-4 and Claude-3 are less vulnerable, but even for them the ASR under ASA is 42 .9–45 .7%, which cannot be considered acceptable. Trojans remain an unsolved problem. Recall, computed through BLEU matching of predicted and true triggers, reaches only ≈ 0.17 against a random-match baseline of 0 .14. At the same time, REASR is close to 0 .99. The gap between the two metrics has a practical meaning: it is easy to find some input that makes the model generate the target output, but it is practically impossible to reconstruct the original triggers with existing methods. Trigger reverse engineering in its current state is unsuitable for model auditing. Heterogeneous committees of 5–7 models were the most effective of the defense mechanisms. They reduced the ASR of ASA by 47–55 pp relative to the most vulnerable undefended model, Gemma-3-4B-Instruct (73.8%). Two factors limit their applicability: the linear growth of computational cost and the dependence on the diversity of the composition. Preliminary observations indicate that homogeneous committees of models from one family gain less because of correlated errors, but a quantitative estimate of this effect requires a separate study. Determining the minimum level of architectural diversity needed for a stable ASR reduction remains an open problem. The validation of the metrics on GPT-4o-mini gave the approximate value ̂ Rstab (f ) = 0 .115 with Rclass (h) = 0 .050. The generative metric captures distributional instability that is invisible in a discrete evaluation. The experiment is limited to 20 prompts with benign perturbations and the top-20 approximation of logprobs. Extending the sample and testing on adversarial scenarios is the immediate next task. 71 Chapter 5 

# Software Implementation of the Suite for Evaluating and Improving LLM Robustness 

# Chapter Overview 

The metrics and algorithms of Chapters 2–3 require software tools that automate attack generation, the collection of model responses, and the computation of robustness indicators. For this purpose, two registered software suites were developed. The JudgeGuard suite (software registration certificate No. 2025687702) evaluates the robustness of judge models to adversarial attacks. The TrojanArmor suite (certificate No. 2025684247) detects and analyzes trojans in large language models. Together, these suites cover the full security evaluation cycle: from the generation of attack inputs and the collection of model responses to the computation of robustness metrics. Section 5.1 presents the modular architecture of both suites. Section 5.2 justifies the choice of the technology stack. Section 5.3 describes the extension of the suites to agentic LLM systems through the MCPSec module, and Section 5.4 reports quantitative characteristics of computational performance. 

# 5.1 Architectural Model of the JudgeGuard and TrojanArmor Software Suites 

The JudgeGuard and TrojanArmor suites solve different tasks. JudgeGuard evaluates the ro-bustness of judge models in the LLM-as-a-Judge architecture to adversarial attacks, whereas TrojanArmor targets the detection and analysis of trojans, also known as backdoors, in large lan-guage models. Despite this difference, both suites rely on a common four-component architecture with a separation of responsibilities between modules. 

5.1.1 Modular Structure of the System 

The architecture of each suite includes four main modules: 1. Attack Generator Module (AGM). The module creates malicious prompts, trigger sequences, and adversarial examples. In JudgeGuard, it implements evolutionary prompt optimization with the ASA algorithm described in Section 3.4.2 of Chapter 3, context injections, and Comparative Undermining Attacks (CUA). In TrojanArmor, the module generates candidate triggers for trigger reconstruction with the gradient-based GCG, PEZ, and GBDA algorithms. For broader applicability, the TrojanArmor suite additionally 72 supports attacks on computer vision models, such as BadNet, Blended, and WaNet, but this thesis uses only the LLM components. 2. Model Router / API Gateway (MRAG). It provides a unified access interface to local models through vLLM or the HuggingFace pipeline and to the cloud APIs of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. The router implements load balancing, retries when API rate limits are exceeded, response caching, and interaction logging. The routing configuration is specified declaratively in YAML format. 3. Evaluation Engine (EE). It computes the quantitative characteristics of robustness and attack effectiveness introduced in Chapter 4: ASR, TSR, Recall, REASR, and the aggregated RUT metric. Architecturally, the module is a set of independent evaluator functions. New metrics are added without changes to existing code, which follows the open/closed principle. 4. Storage Layer (SL). It stores intermediate and final experimental results. Two operating modes are provided. File storage in JSON/JSONL format targets reproducibility and ver-sioning of results. Relational storage based on PostgreSQL supports analytical SQL queries over metrics. Each record carries experiment metadata: the run identifier, timestamps, the model configuration, and the generation parameters.

# 5.3 Module for Evaluating the Robustness of Agentic LLM Sys-tems (MCPSec) 

Agentic LLM systems form input sequences from the data of external tools through the Model Context Protocol (MCP) [35]. To test such systems, the MCPSec module [11] was developed and integrated into the four-component architecture described in Section 5.1. As part of the integration, the AGM attack generator was extended with MCP injection templates, the MRAG router received adapters for the MCP transport, and the EE module was extended with specialized security metrics for agentic interaction. The MCP architecture and the testing mechanisms are described below, followed by the defense algorithms. 

5.3.1 Architecture of the Model Context Protocol 

The Model Context Protocol defines a client-server architecture in which the LLM agent acts as the client and interacts with a set of servers that provide access to tools, data sources, and prompts. The protocol is based on the JSON-RPC 2.0 specification with two supported transport mechanisms: • stdio (Standard I/O) : a transport over the standard input and output streams of a child process, intended for local servers launched as child processes of the host application. • SSE (Server-Sent Events) : an asynchronous HTTP transport for remote servers with support for streaming data transfer. The main protocol methods include: • tools/list : retrieves the list of available tools with descriptions and JSON schemas of their parameters. 75 • tools/call : invokes a tool with the given arguments and returns a CallToolResult object with the result. • prompts/list : retrieves the list of available prompt templates. • resources/read : reads data from resources provided by the server.

5.3.3 Protocol Vulnerabilities: From Prompt Injection to Tool Hijacking 

MCP contains a critical vulnerability at the boundary between natural language processing and program code execution. The LLM agent decides which tool to call based on a context that may include data from untrusted sources. This creates the attack vector known as Prompt Injection to Tool Hijacking. An analysis of the MCP specification revealed three architectural vulnerabilities: 1. Capability Attestation Absence. MCP servers declare their own capabilities when the connection is initialized, and neither the client nor a third party verifies them. The protocol allows capabilities to change during a session without validation. A compromised server can escalate its privileges from restricted to unrestricted after the connection is established. 2. Unauthenticated Sampling. The sampling mechanism allows servers to request the generation of LLM responses while assigning the “user” role to messages. The protocol does not authenticate the message source, and the user cannot visually distinguish messages injected by the server from their own requests. This opens a channel for direct protocol-level prompt injection. 76 3. Implicit Trust Propagation. In configurations with several MCP servers, isolation boundaries are not defined. Tool responses from one server influence tool calls to another without tracking data provenance. A compromise of one server propagates privileges in a cascade through all connected services. The context of an LLM agent consists of the system prompt, the user request, and the results of previous tool calls. An adversary who controls call results through a compromised MCP server injects a malicious instruction into the returned data. The LLM agent does not distinguish instructions from data, so the modified context forces it to call an arbitrary tool with malicious arguments. Threat 1 in Section 1.2.2 of Chapter 1 formalizes prompt injection. The mechanism above is its indirect variant: the injected instruction arrives through tool data, not through the user query. In the MCP context, however, the consequences are more severe: tool calls lead to arbitrary code execution, file system modification, and outgoing network requests, and the side effects reach the real execution environment. The software suite implements a module for automated testing of MCP servers for robustness to this class of attacks. The module generates malicious tool responses, tracks the subsequent agent calls, and evaluates whether the hijacking succeeds.

5.3.5 Algorithmic Support for the Security of Agentic Interaction 

Based on the MCP vulnerabilities identified in Section 5.3.3, the MCPSec module includes two algorithms for the software defense of agentic systems. The first, AttestMCP, implements cryptographic attestation of messages. The second, the Commit Boundary pattern, isolates code execution. 77 Algorithm for Cryptographic Attestation and Message Routing (AttestMCP) 

The AttestMCP algorithm restricts the tool calls available to the LLM agent to those listed in a permission table fixed in advance, and it protects the integrity of each RPC packet with an HMAC signature. The defense rests on two distinct layers. The permission table defines authorization: it specifies which tools, and with which arguments, are allowed in the session. The HMAC signature ensures integrity: it authenticates the packet source at the transport layer and protects the packet from modification in transit. Let P = {(si, t j , a k)} be the permission table, where si is the session identifier, tj is the tool identifier, and ak is the specification of allowed arguments. Without loss of generality, P holds at most one entry per session–tool pair, because several argument specifications for one pair merge into their union. The table is built during the session initialization phase, before data from untrusted sources enter the agent context. During the execution phase, P remains unchanged, and the LLM agent has no operation that extends it. This restriction of the reachable agent actions to P is the main defense mechanism, and it does not depend on the context content. Each RPC packet carries a signature σ = HMAC( Ksession , s i∥tj ∥hash(args)), where Ksession

Complexity analysis. Computing HMAC-SHA256 takes O(|args |). The lookup of an entry in the permission table, implemented with a hash table, runs in amortized O(1). Argument validation against the JSON schema requires O(|args | · | schema |) comparison operations in the worst case, which is linear in |args | for a fixed schema. In practice, the total overhead did not exceed 0.1 ms per call for |args | ≤ 4096 bytes. Measurements were taken on an Intel Xeon Gold 6248R CPU with a clock frequency of 3.0 GHz, and LLM inference ran on an NVIDIA A100. The attestation time was three orders of magnitude lower than the typical inference latency of 300–800 ms for models of the LLaMA-2-13B class. 

Limits of the guarantees. The authorization and integrity layers solve different tasks, and their functions are not interchangeable. The HMAC signature confirms the source and integrity of a packet, but not the intent of the call. A tool call that the LLM agent forms under the influence of an injection passes through the same legitimate client as a call made at the user’s request, and it receives a valid signature. The signature by itself does not separate a call 78 authorized by the user from a call imposed by an injection. The permission table provides this separation: an injection does not take the agent outside P, because the table is fixed before untrusted data arrive and does not change during the session. The cryptographic layer closes a separate vector: packet forgery within the cross-server trust propagation from Section 5.3.3. A compromised MCP server does not hold the key Ksession ,which is stored at the transport layer outside the context window of the model. Such a server cannot forge a packet on behalf of the client or of another server, which blocks privilege escalation through forged packets. A tool call that the agent itself forms after reading a malicious response from a compromised server still receives a valid signature. The permission table P, not the signature, bounds such calls. AttestMCP leaves part of the risk in place. If an injection forces the agent to call an allowed tool with allowed arguments for a malicious purpose, the packet passes both checks. The argument specification ak and the Commit Boundary pattern from Section 5.3.5 narrow this residual risk: the narrower the allowed set of arguments, the smaller the space for abuse within P.

Components of the Integrated Defense in the MCPSec Module 

The MCPSec module [11] combines the AttestMCP and Commit Boundary algorithms with four additional defense mechanisms. 

Capability Certificates restrict the allowed set of operations of each server and thereby prevent privilege escalation. Origin Tagging separates message sources: a visual and programmatic tag distinguishes the output of the sampling mechanism from user requests. Without explicit user permission, the Isolation Enforcement mechanism blocks cross-server data transfer. Replay Protection based on timestamps and one-time nonces prevents the replay of intercepted messages. On the MCPBench benchmark, MCPSec reduced the average attack success rate from 53.7% to 12.4%, that is, by 41.3 pp. The additional steady-state processing latency per message for attestation and the MCPSec filtering mechanisms did not exceed 8.3 ms. In the evaluation of model ensembles, described as committees in Section 3.4.1 of Chapter 3, the scalability of the system with a growing number of models k becomes a critical parameter. The MRAG router implements two operating modes: • Parallel inference (GPU sharing): several models are placed on one GPU with the NVIDIA Multi-Process Service (MPS) mechanism. Throughput scales sublinearly: Θk ≈ Θ1 · k0.7, where Θ k is the total throughput for k models and Θ 1 is the throughput for a single model. • Distributed inference (multi-GPU): each model is placed on a separate GPU. Through-put scales linearly: Θ k ≈ k·Θ1, subject to the interconnect communication overhead ( ∼2–5% loss with NVLink). 82 The experiments described in Chapter 4 used a configuration with 4 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, which can simultaneously serve up to 4 models with 13B parameters or 2 models with 70B parameters when tensor parallelism is used. In the distributed mode, where each model is placed on a separate GPU, the linear estimate gives Θ 4 ≈ 4 · 14 .1 = 56 .4 req./s, or about 54–55 req./s after the 2–5% interconnect loss. The measured total throughput reached up to 45 model inference requests per second for four 13B models. The overhead of request orchestration and of the synchronization of committee verdicts explains the gap from the linear estimate. The evaluation of a dataset of 10,000 prompts with a committee of k models generates 10 ,000 · k

model requests, in particular 30 ,000 for k = 3. For k = 4, the 40 ,000 requests at the measured throughput of about 45 req./s take ∼15 minutes. For k = 3, the committee occupies three of the four GPUs, so the throughput scales to about 3 /4 · 45 ≈ 34 req./s and the 30 ,000 requests also take ∼15 minutes. The time drops to ∼11 minutes only if all four GPUs serve the three-model committee, for example with a replica of one model.

# Chapter Overview 

A number of practical tasks separate laboratory experiments from production systems: the choice of metrics for deployment effectiveness, the reconciliation of security requirements with the computational budget, and the adaptation of defenses to the specifics of the application domain. The methodology for deploying defense mechanisms relies on technical and economic evaluation metrics. The practical examples for NLP systems and safety-critical applications are complemented by an analysis of technology development trends. 

# 6.1 Methodology for Deploying Robustness Enhancement Meth-ods 

The deployment of LLM robustness enhancement methods in real systems is not limited to the application of individual algorithms. It requires a systematic approach that takes into account the specifics of the task, the available resources, and the security requirements. The discrete token space of LLMs imposes additional constraints on the choice and adaptation of methods. 

6.1.1 Stages of Deploying Robustness Enhancement Methods 

We propose a deployment methodology based on the life cycle of risk management and secure software development [46, 138]. The methodology comprises five consecutive stages. 

1. Requirements analysis and risk assessment. The robustness and security require-ments of the system are determined from its purpose and the potential consequences of failures. Risk assessment uses standard methodologies, in particular the NIST AI Risk Management Framework [46] and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM [45], described in Chapter 1. At the same stage, the relevant threats listed in Section 1.2.2 of Chapter 1 are identified: prompt injection, including attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge and on defended systems, and trojan attacks, taking into account how hard they are to detect. The result of this stage is recorded as a prioritized list of risks: 

Rpriority = Prioritize( {(ai, v j , P exploit (vj |ai), ImpactValue( vj )) }),

where ai ∈ A type is an attack type, vj ∈ V is a vulnerability, Pexploit is the probability of exploitation, and ImpactValue is a quantitative estimate of the damage, as defined in Defini-tions 1.3 and 1.4 of Chapter 1. The criticality of each attack-vulnerability pair is computed as 

cij = Pexploit (vj |ai) · ImpactValue( vj ) and is used for prioritization. 85 Remark 6.1. Empirical observation: limited effectiveness of defenses against unknown attacks. The effectiveness of defense and robustness enhancement methods developed and tested on a known set of attacks Aknown can decrease when they face new, unforeseen attacks Anew under real conditions. The degree of this decrease depends on the ability of the defense mechanism to generalize to new types of attacks [139]. Even general defense principles have limits of generalization if an attack targets vulnerabilities that were not considered in the design of the defense mechanism. Formally, if RA(f ) is a robustness or security metric with respect to the attacks from A, then the empirically observed tendency is RAnew (f ) ≤ RAknown (f ). The decrease ( RAknown (f ) − RAnew (f )) depends on the “distance” between Anew and Aknown and on the generalization ability of the defense. 

Justification. Defense mechanisms are often optimized to counter specific patterns or characteristics of known attacks. New attacks can use other vectors or bypass specific assumptions made during the development of the defense. The generalization ability of a defense depends on the principles built into it. For example, a Lipschitz constraint generalizes better than filters based on signatures of known attacks. 

2. Selection of robustness enhancement methods. Based on the results of the risk analysis, the defense and robustness enhancement methods described in Chapter 3 are selected. The selection takes the following factors into account: • The effectiveness of the method against the prioritized risks. The experimental data of Chapter 4 confirm the effectiveness of committees of models against attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge. • Compatibility with the architecture and size of the target model. • The ratio of the computational requirements to the available resources. The requirements are expressed through the computational overhead: ∆Cost in Chapter 4 and CO in Section 5.4.4 of Chapter 5. • The impact on model utility. The acceptable bounds are determined by the RUT metric from Chapter 4. The experimental data of Chapter 4 and the principle of multi-layer defense from Section 3.2.2 of Chapter 3 support the combination of methods. Formally, the selection reduces to an optimization problem: maximize the reduction of the total risk under a limited budget. In practice, the solution often requires expert judgment. 

3. Implementation and integration. The selected methods are implemented and integrated into the system. At this stage, where needed, the model architecture is modified according to Section 3.2 of Chapter 3, robust training or fine-tuning is performed according to Section 3.3, and data pre- and post-processing modules are built: input filters and sanitizers, and output verifiers according to Section 3.2.2. In parallel, the generation parameters are configured and monitoring systems are connected. 

4. Testing and validation. The deployed defense mechanisms are evaluated with the metrics from Chapter 4: robustness to known attacks by ASR, trojan detection quality by Recall and REASR, utility on legitimate queries by U-metrics, and the robustness-utility trade-off by RUT. In parallel, red teaming [89, 90] is carried out to find new vulnerabilities, including automated approaches validated in the Kaggle competitions and the SaTML CTF. The testing results guide the iterative refinement of the defense. 

5. Monitoring and updating. After deployment, data on queries, responses, and defense triggers are collected continuously. The threat profile is tracked through new publications and competition results. Periodic re-testing and red teaming reveal degradation of the defense. 6.1.2 Evaluation of Deployment Effectiveness 

The evaluation of deployment effectiveness is not limited to technical security indicators. It also covers the impact on performance and costs. 

1. Technical effectiveness metrics. Robustness and security are evaluated through ASR, Recall, and REASR (Section 4.1.1 of Chapter 4). For each technical metric C, the difference from the base model is recorded as ∆ C = C(fdef ) − C(fbase ). Utility is monitored with BLEU, ROUGE, accuracy, and BERTScore on clean data: ∆ U = U (fdef ) − U (fbase ). Performance is characterized by the computational overhead CO (Section 5.4.4 of Chapter 5), together with memory usage and system throughput.

The evaluation must be repeated regularly, since the threat landscape changes, and without revision the defense mechanisms become obsolete. 

# 6.2 Examples of Practical Application 

The practical application of the results of this work takes two forms. Completed deployments confirm that the methods work in practice. Typical reference scenarios are recommendations. They illustrate how the developed approaches transfer to other application domains. The completed deployments are as follows. The vulnerability evaluation framework, the committee-of-models method, and the methodology of multi-step attacks are applied in the production environment of Viasat to secure a recommendation ranking module built on the LLM-as-a-Judge architecture. As part of this work at Viasat, the JudgeGuard suite was installed to test the recommendation ranking module with prompt injection methods, including the ASA algorithm developed by the author and CUA attacks. A committee of three models (GPT-4, Claude-3-Opus, Llama 3) uses majority voting to produce the final ranking verdicts. Load testing confirmed that the latency increase is acceptable: no more than 15% compared with a single model. According to internal testing at Viasat, the ASR decreased by 42 percentage points. This result was obtained on the company’s internal dataset, which differs from the experimental sample of Chapter 4. In addition, input filters based on a Python sanitizer were deployed to block injections in user reviews that enter the evaluation module. At the time of writing, the validation of the TrojanArmor suite and the MCPSec module is limited to the TDC2023 and MCPBench benchmarks, respectively. Unlike JudgeGuard, these tools were not deployed in production, since trojan detection and the protection of agentic MCP systems were not part of the risk profile of the production site considered. The developed methods are applicable in different application domains. The typical scenarios and reference architectures below describe possible deployment schemes. 

6.2.1 Application in Natural Language Processing Systems 

The examples in this and the following subsection are recommendations. They describe typical reference architectures, not deployed systems. The completed deployments are listed at the beginning of this section. 

Natural language processing systems differ in their threat profiles. We consider three types of such systems and the approaches to their security: chatbots and virtual assistants, content 88 generation systems, and evaluation and recommendation systems. The last two types share a threat surface and are treated together. 

Chatbots and Virtual Assistants 

As a reference scenario, we consider a customer support system of a financial organization. Users ask questions about products, rates and account transactions through a text interface. The threat profile includes prompt injections (LLM01 in OWASP, Threat 1), bypassing restrictions through jailbreaking (Threat 14), leakage of confidential data from the dialogue context (LLM06, Threat 11), and targeted manipulation of behavior. The central element of the defense is context isolation [86], which prevents the model from interpreting user data as instructions. Before the input reaches the model, input filters, both lexical and LLM-based semantic ones, screen out dangerous patterns. The model level relies on safety alignment through RLHF/RLAIF [36, 83], described in Section 3.3 of Chapter 3, and on explicit restrictions in the system prompt. PII is removed before it is passed to the LLM. The model is fine-tuned through RLHF so that it does not generate unverified financial advice. Dialogue logging makes it possible to detect new attack vectors. 

Content Generation, Evaluation and Recommendation 

The outputs of generative LLM applications and of evaluation/recommendation systems directly affect the decisions of users or other systems, which creates a common threat surface. Malicious code (LLM02 in OWASP), disinformation, and ranking manipulation through adversarial queries form the main threats. In LLM-as-a-Judge systems, CUA and JMA attacks, studied in Chapter 4, distort the verdicts directly. Recommender systems are vulnerable to leakage of user preferences (LLM06 in OWASP) and to the amplification of filter bubbles. Generated content is checked by external verifiers [141, 142] and by static code analysis [143]. Constrained decoding [144] narrows the set of admissible outputs. Robust training (Section 3.3 of Chapter 3) reduces the sensitivity of the model to provocative queries. In ranking systems, robust embeddings [145] together with anomaly detectors form the first layer. It is complemented by outlier-insensitive algorithms [146], fair ML techniques [147], and differential privacy [148]. Against CUA and JMA attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge, committees of several models and a comparative evaluation format are effective. Two reference scenarios show how the emphasis differs. A platform for grading student essays uses a committee of three LLMs: the essays pass a perplexity check and instruction filtering and are then evaluated in a comparative format against reference works. The recommender system of a news portal is organized differently: an ensemble of independent models is combined with filters for adversarial patterns at the input and with algorithms that ensure content diversity. The choice of specific defense methods is determined by the specifics of the application: excessive protection reduces functionality, and insufficient protection creates vulnerabilities. 

6.2.2 Application in Critical Systems 

Safety-critical systems impose stricter reliability requirements. The vulnerabilities to prompt injection and hidden trojans described in the previous chapters make the use of LLMs in such systems risky without special measures. 

Medical Decision Support Systems 

Reference scenario: an LLM analyzes X-ray images and relies on a verified knowledge base of pathologies through RAG [149]. A second independent algorithm checks the conclusions before they are presented to the physician, and the results are accompanied by a confidence estimate and references to sources. The architecture counters the main risks of medical LLM 89 applications: incorrect diagnoses, leakage of patient data (PHI), poisoning of training data [150], and demographic bias. The cost of an error in the medical domain is higher than in most other domains. The defense therefore relies on several principles at once. Where possible, certified robustness methods with mathematical guarantees are applied [151]. The physician remains the final link in decision making (human-in-the-loop). The explainability of conclusions is achieved with explainable AI methods [152]. Bias auditing [153] and data protection according to HIPAA requirements complement the technical measures. 

Financial Systems and Infrastructure Management 

The common principle for financial and infrastructure LLM applications is that the model is isolated from making autonomous decisions with irreversible consequences. The nature of the threats and the regulatory context differ, however. In finance, LLM applications are exposed to the generation of erroneous forecasts with direct losses, to the manipulation of input data through news and social networks, and to leakage of insider information. Ensembling of heterogeneous models with diversified sources, anomaly detectors [154] and robust time series forecasting methods [155] form the technical basis. Regulatory monitoring follows SOX, GDPR, and industry regulations. As a reference scenario, we consider an algorithmic trading system. Multi-layer filters screen out unreliable sources, trade decisions are made on the basis of a consensus of several models with confirmation by classical quantitative indicators, and the whole system runs in an isolated environment. In the management of power grids, transport and industrial equipment, the nature of the threats is different: incorrect control commands and targeted attacks create physical safety risks. The LLM is limited to an advisory role and is architecturally separated from the direct control of critical components. The model’s recommendations undergo formal verification [156] and are approved by an operator. Network segmentation, multi-level redundancy, intrusion detection, and safe shutdown mechanisms for anomalies form additional lines of defense. In all critical systems, technical measures are effective only in combination with organizational processes, periodic audits, and compliance with regulations. 

# 6.3 Prospects for the Development of Robustness Enhancement Methods 

The field of LLM security changes quickly: over 2023–2025, more than 400 papers on attacks on language models and more than 200 on defense mechanisms were published. New attack techniques appear more often than defense updates are released. 

6.3.1 Research Trends and Directions 

Theoretical Foundations and Formal Verification 

Formal models do not keep pace with attack practice: the gap between experimental results and the theoretical understanding of LLM vulnerabilities persists. A complete formalization of robustness in high-dimensional discrete spaces remains an open problem. Mathematical models that adequately describe the space of tokens and semantic transformations have not yet been developed. Provable guarantees for most defense methods used with LLMs, including alignment techniques such as RLHF, do not yet exist. The quantitative analysis of the robustness-utility, privacy-utility, and fairness-robustness trade-offs is far from complete, and the results for the RUT metric from Chapter 4 only confirm the significance of these trade-offs. The formal verification of LLM properties [156, 157] is a separate task. SMT solvers and abstract interpretation are so far applicable only to small networks. Progress in scaling abstract 90 interpretation nevertheless suggests that the verification of individual properties will become feasible for models with billions of parameters as well. 

From Reactive to Adaptive Defense 

Existing defenses are built for known classes of attacks and remain reactive. The experience of the SaTML CTF and TDC2023 demonstrates that attackers systematically bypass fixed filters. Next-generation defenses must reconfigure themselves when they encounter zero-day attacks. Online training of defense modules on a stream of new attacks appears to be a promising direction. Self-healing architectures develop this idea by automatically closing detected vulnerabilities. Red teaming with adaptive attacks such as ASA already makes it possible to automate security testing. Game-theoretic modeling [64] provides a tool for optimizing defense strategies. All these approaches, however, place high demands on computational resources. Adaptivity and interpretability are directly related. Without an understanding of the causes of a vulnerability, a defense cannot generalize to new attacks. The problem of unintended triggers in trojan attacks, that is, surrogate inputs that differ from the true trigger but still activate the backdoor (Section 4.2.2 of Chapter 4), illustrates the lack of interpretability. Methods that localize vulnerable components can identify the specific layers and attention heads responsible for backdoor behavior [158]. Explainable defense mechanisms that report the reason for blocking a query and the visualization of attacks through JailbreakLens [159] complement this approach. 

Multimodal Robustness and New Attack Surfaces 

GPT-4V, Gemini 1.5, and LLaVA are already deployed in production systems and accept multimodal input [160, 161]. New modalities open new attack surfaces. Cross-modal influence makes it possible, for example, to change the text output of a model through a specially crafted image in a document. Defense mechanisms for multimodal systems do not yet form an established class. The development of robustness metrics that account for perturbations in each modality separately and in their combination remains an open problem. Two related directions widen the threat landscape beyond multimodal robustness. Federated training of LLMs [162] creates the threat of poisoning through the aggregation of participant updates. A network participant can implant a backdoor into the global model, and protocols such as FedAvg are not robust to such influence [163]. Differential privacy does not remove this threat. The norm clipping and noise of participant-level differential privacy limit a backdoor only at a cost in accuracy [164]. This cost falls unevenly on underrepresented subgroups [78]. The noise also makes malicious updates harder to distinguish from benign ones. Quantum computers pose a threat of a different nature. Grover’s algorithm reduces the cost of a brute-force search for a 256-bit HMAC-SHA256 key from 2 256 to 2 128 operations [165]. A 128-bit security level remains adequate for symmetric primitives, so the HMAC in AttestMCP from Chapter 5 does not need replacement. Post-quantum adaptation concerns the public-key mechanisms that establish the session key Ksession , if these rely on classical asymmetric cryptography. The question of whether quantum algorithms can speed up the search for adversarial examples for neural networks still has no theoretical estimates. 

6.3.2 Technology Development Forecasts 

Based on current trends, several probable directions of development can be identified. The most evident one is the escalation of automated attacks and defenses. LLM agents are already used for automated red teaming [44]. Adaptive attacks such as ASA from Chapter 3 demonstrate how evolutionary search bypasses static filters. In response, defenses will use language models themselves for threat detection [166]. Platforms such as Gandalf [167] already implement LLM-based generation of attack scenarios. The results of the SaTML CTF confirm the intensification of this “race”. 91 In parallel, robustness requirements will shift to the early stages of LLM development according to the “security by design” principle, and the after-the-fact addition of filters will give way to frameworks with security guarantees at the training stage. HELM [21], JailbreakBench [168], and MT-Bench form the basis for standardized comparative evaluation. The TDC2023 and Kaggle competitions accelerate this process. For critical applications, model certification by analogy with Common Criteria is likely. In this context, RLHF/RLAIF [36, 83] and adversarial training from Chapter 3 solve related tasks by different means, and their integration will yield models that are both aligned with human preferences and robust to manipulation. Industry specialization and formal verification deserve separate attention. Universal methods will give way to specialized solutions: Mixture-of-Experts architectures will require protection of the expert router, financial [169] and medical [153] applications will dictate their own verification requirements, and the protection of LLM-as-a-Judge will become an independent research direction. In parallel, the need for mathematical security guarantees will grow [157]. Scaling formal methods to models with billions of parameters is not yet solved, but progress in the abstract interpretation of neural networks gives grounds for optimism. The general trend is that security and robustness are gradually being built into the main LLM development cycle, and reactive measures are giving way to proactive ones. 

# Chapter Conclusions 

The transfer of the results into production revealed a limitation that is not visible on benchmarks: the effect of a defense depends on the data of the specific deployment. The deployment of a committee of three models at Viasat reduced the ASR by 42 pp on an internal dataset different from the experimental sample of Chapter 4, with a latency increase of no more than 15%. This confirms the transferability of the method but shows that the numerical indicators of a defense do not transfer between domains without recalibration. The assessment of production readiness requires metrics of three groups: technical, economic, and integral metrics such as RUT. The integral metric links heterogeneous criteria and makes it possible to recompute the optimal defense configuration when the threat landscape changes, whereas the technical and economic metrics taken separately do not allow such a recomputation. The cost of a committee is linear in the number of models, but parallel querying of the members makes the actual latency increase sublinear. To reduce costs further, a cascade scheme is promising: a smaller committee, for example of three models, processes the query, and the full committee is activated only at a low level of agreement, which is detected by the confidence 

c from Section 3.4.1 of Chapter 3. A quantitative estimate of the savings remains an open problem. 92 Conclusion 

This thesis develops methods for evaluating and improving the robustness of large language models to input sequence variations. The main results are as follows. 1. The analysis of the Trojan Detection Challenge 2023 data revealed an asymmetry between the generation and the detection of trojan triggers. Surrogate triggers activate the trojan almost always: REASR ≈ 0.99. The original trigger is not reconstructed: the Recall metric, a BLEU-based similarity between predicted and true triggers, did not exceed ≈ 0.17 even for the best method, GCG. This gap argues for prioritizing preventive measures: auditing the training data and controlling the supply chain. The result relates to Objectives 1–2. 2. The generative robustness metric Rstab (f ) accounts for the discreteness of the token space by capturing the per-step divergence of output distributions under small input perturbations. For localized attacks, the bound V (h) ≤ 1 − Rclass (h) is proved. For non-localized attacks, a parametric family V (h) ≈ g(1 − Rclass (h)) with parameters calibrated on data is proposed as an empirical hypothesis. The result relates to Objective 2. 3. The vulnerability of LLM-as-a-Judge systems to prompt injections is studied within the developed framework, which divides attacks into content attacks and instructive attacks. The adaptive generation algorithm ASA achieves an ASR of up to 73.8% on the Gemma, Llama, GPT-4, and Claude-3-Opus models. The vulnerability depends on the architectural family and the alignment procedure, and not only on the model scale. The attacks transfer well between open architectures. The result relates to Objective 3. 4. Based on SaTML CTF 2024 data, multi-step attacks on LLMs with a three-layer defense are systematized. Four bypass vectors are classified: code-oriented attacks, ASCII encoding, word splitting, and contextual misdirection. The first three are neutralized by input normalization, and the fourth requires contextual isolation of system instructions. As a defense mechanism, committees of 5–7 heterogeneous models are developed. For the most vulnerable model, Gemma-3-4B, the ASR reduction was 47–55 percentage points. The theoretical basis is the Condorcet jury theorem: if the committee members err independently and each has a base accuracy r > 0.5, the accuracy of the majority verdict grows with the size of the committee. This result concerns committee accuracy and does not by itself guarantee robustness in the sense of Rclass . For Gemma-3-4B with r ≈ 0.26, the condition of the theorem does not hold, and the ASR reduction is achieved empirically through the heterogeneity of the committee composition, which decorrelates the errors of models of different architectures. The robustness-utility trade-off metric RUT for a committee of 7 models is ≈ 5.5. The result relates to Objective 4. 5. Architectural vulnerabilities of MCP are identified that enable attacks of the prompt injection to tool hijacking class. To counter them, two algorithms are proposed. AttestMCP restricts tool calls to a session permission table and checks packet integrity with HMAC. Commit Boundary isolates the execution of the agent’s code. On the MCPBench benchmark of 847 scenarios, the average ASR decreased by 41.3 percentage points, from 53.7% to 12.4%. The result relates to Objective 5. 93 6. The JudgeGuard and TrojanArmor software suites are implemented. They cover the full cycle of LLM security testing, from the generation of attacks to the computation of robustness metrics. The MCPSec module complements both suites with tools for the analysis of agentic LLM systems. The result relates to Objective 5. The practical significance of the results is confirmed by the deployment of the JudgeGuard suite and the defense method based on model committees in the production environment of Viasat. The proposed metrics, attack and defense algorithms, and software suites are applicable to existing and future LLM systems. Directions for future work include the extension of the framework to multimodal models, the adaptation of the algorithms to real-time attack detection, and the construction of verifiable security guarantees. 94 Acknowledgements

99 Glossary 

Large language model (LLM) A class of machine learning models based on deep neural networks trained on large volumes of text data for understanding, generating, and processing natural language. 

Token The smallest unit of text that a language model operates on, for example a word, a part of a word, or a character. The process of converting text into a sequence of tokens is called tokenization. 

Embedding A vector representation of a token, phrase, or document in a high-dimensional space that reflects its semantic properties. 

Robustness The ability of a model, or of a compositional system based on it, to preserve correct behavior and acceptable quality characteristics under small perturbations of input data, adversarial modifications of queries, and other influences considered in the adopted threat model. It is the main term of this work. When the work refers to the literature on adversarial robustness, the term is used in the same sense. The robustness metric Rstab (f ) quantifies robustness through the divergence of output distributions. Its subscript comes from “stability” and does not imply a separate notion of stability. 

Vulnerability A property of a system or model that an attacker can exploit to disrupt its normal operation, compromise data, or bypass defense mechanisms. 

Attack A targeted influence on a system or model intended to cause undesirable behavior, gain unauthorized access, extract information, or disrupt the operation of the target. 

Adversarial attack A type of attack that adds small perturbations, often imperceptible to a human, to the input data and thereby causes erroneous or undesirable behavior of the model. 

Adversarial example Input data modified as a result of an adversarial attack. 

Jailbreaking A type of attack aimed at bypassing the safety mechanisms built into an LLM (safety alignment) in order to make the model generate prohibited, harmful, or unethical content. 

Prompt injection A type of attack in which hidden or disguised instructions are injected into a legitimate user query, or into external data that the model processes, such as documents or tool outputs (indirect prompt injection), and override the original system instructions of the model or its safety mechanisms. 

Data poisoning An attack in which the adversary injects a small number of specially crafted (“poisoned”) examples into the training dataset in order to disrupt the training process, degrade the quality of the model, create trojans, or amplify bias. 

Trojan (backdoor) Hidden functionality embedded in a model, typically through data poisoning. The trojan is activated by a special trigger in the input data and causes behavior predefined by the attacker. In this work, the main term is “trojan”, and “backdoor” is used as a synonym. 

Alignment The process of training or fine-tuning an LLM in order to bring its behavior in line with human values, ethical norms, and instructions, and to prevent the generation of harmful, biased, or undesirable content. 

Differential privacy A formal definition of privacy that guarantees that the probability distribution of the result of a randomized data analysis changes very little if any single record is removed from or changed in the dataset. It is used to protect the confidentiality of individual data in training datasets. 100 Levenshtein distance A metric that measures the minimal number of single-symbol edit operations (insertion, deletion, substitution) required to transform one string of characters or tokens into another. 

KL divergence (Kullback–Leibler divergence) An asymmetric measure of divergence between two probability distributions. This work mainly uses its symmetric counterpart, the Jensen–Shannon divergence DJS , which is built from two KL terms. 

Committee of models (ensemble of models) An approach to improving the reliability and accuracy of predictions in which several independently trained models are used and their outputs are combined, for example through voting, averaging, or a weighted combination, to obtain the final result. In this work, a committee of heterogeneous LLMs aggregates the verdicts of judge models by majority voting, which reduces the success of attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge. Low agreement among the members flags a potentially malicious query for manual review. 

LLM-as-a-Judge An architecture or methodology of automatic evaluation in which a large language model acts as an evaluator and issues a discrete or ranked verdict on the quality, safety, instruction compliance, or preferability of the responses of another model or of several alternative responses. 

Multi-layer defense (defense in depth) A security strategy that applies several indepen-dent layers of defense mechanisms. In the context of LLMs, it includes a combination of different methods: input filtering, monitoring of model behavior, post-processing of output data, model alignment, and other techniques that work together to minimize risks. 101 Bibliography
