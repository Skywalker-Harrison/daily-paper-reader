# AccentCL: Robust Accent Classification with Incremental Expansion 

Mu-Ruei Tseng, Waris Quamer, Ghady Nasrallah & Ricardo Gutierrez-Osuna 

Department of Computer Science and Engineering Texas A&M University 

College Station, TX 77840, USA 

{mtseng, quamer.waris, ghadynasrallah, rgutier }@tamu.edu 

Abstract —Accent classifiers are typically trained with a fixed label inventory and cannot accommodate new accent categories as new data becomes available. Moreover, accented speech corpora often exhibit substantial class imbalance and/or domain shift due to differences in recording conditions across corpora. We present AccentCL, a class-incremental learning framework for English accent classification that is robust to class imbalance and cross-corpus domain shift. AccentCL extracts multi-layer representations from a frozen Whisper-Large-v3 encoder, opti-mized with an imbalance-aware cross-entropy loss to reduce bias toward the majority accent classes and a domain mean alignment loss that minimizes distributional mean shift across training corpora. The label space is then expanded via replay-based continual learning, using the frozen base model for knowledge retention and an old-to-new margin loss to reduce overprediction on newly added classes. On a five-class accent classification task, AccentCL achieves 77.1% balanced accuracy and a 76.9% macro-averaged F1 score. We further evaluate the model’s ability to incrementally incorporate two new accent categories: Spanish-accented and Chinese-accented English. When adding Spanish-accented English to the pretrained model, AccentCL attains an F1 of 83.3% on the new class while retaining 77.3% balanced accuracy on the base classes. When subsequently adding Chinese-accented English, it achieves 61.8% F1 on the new class while preserving 77.6% balanced accuracy on the previously learned classes. These results show that AccentCL enables robust regional accent classification while allowing new accent categories to be added without full retraining. 

Index Terms —accent classification, continual learning 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The ability to identify regional or non-native varieties of speech enables a range of applications, including speaker population analysis, evaluation of accented speech synthe-sis/conversion systems [1], [2], and measurement of perfor-mance disparities in downstream speech models [3]. Further, accent-related variation carries acoustic and phonetic cues (e.g., in pronunciation, rhythm, and prosody) that can affect the performance of automatic speech recognition, voice conver-sion, and spoken language understanding systems. Recent accent classifiers [4], [5] benefit from large pre-trained speech encoders and public speech corpora, but often rely on strong assumptions about the underlying data. First, accent labels are typically defined along political or national boundaries (e.g., American vs. Canadian English) rather than geographic or cultural lines, even though intra-national accent variation can be substantial — differences between Southern and Northeastern US accents, for instance, are often more pro-nounced than those between American and Canadian English. Second, accent label distributions tend to be long-tailed, with some categories represented by substantially fewer samples than others. Third, training samples are frequently pooled across multiple corpora that differ in recording conditions, speaker demographics, and collection protocols, introducing corpus-level domain shift. Finally, these models do not allow new accent categories to be added after a classifier has already been deployed. We propose A CCENT CL, a framework for robust, class-incremental English accent classification. AccentCL first trains an accent classifier by merging closely related fine-grained ac-cent labels into broader regional groups that reflect geographic influences, reducing ambiguity arising from acoustically simi-lar or inconsistently annotated labels. The classifier operates on multi-layer representations extracted from a frozen Whisper-Large-v3 encoder and is trained with class-prior adjustment and corpus-mean alignment to improve robustness to class im-balance and cross-corpus domain shift, respectively. AccentCL then expands the label space by incorporating new accent classes, using a small replay memory of previously learned accents stratified across corpora. During continual training, the updated model is regularized against the frozen base classifier for knowledge retention and uses an old-to-new margin loss to reduce overprediction of newly added classes on samples from previously learned accents. Together, these components allow the classifier to learn new accent categories while preserving performance on previously learned ones. A. Accent classification with speech foundation models 

Accent classification aims to identify a speaker’s regional or non-native accent from speech. Recent systems com-monly build on pretrained speech representations, including wav2vec 2.0 [6], XLS-R [7], WavLM [8], and Whisper [9]. These models yield contextual frame-level representations that capture acoustic, phonetic, and speaker-related informa-tion [10], which can then be aggregated via utterance-level pooling and passed to a classification head. CommonAccent [4] is a pretrained English accent clas-sifier trained on CommonVoice [11] with a fixed 16-class accent inventory, but its single-domain training data and fine-grained label space may limit how well it generalizes to other domains. In our evaluation, many low-resource and out-of-domain accents are confused with related labels or absorbed into dominant classes, which motivates our use of a regional label mapping together with domain-aware training. Voxlect [5] introduces a larger mixed-corpus benchmark for English dialect and regional language classification, evaluating several speech foundation models with LoRA fine-tuning; its strongest configuration, based on Whisper-Large-v3, serves as a strong reference model for accent-related classification. However, Voxlect remains a fixed-label classifier and is not built to accommodate new accent categories after training. AccentCL addresses this limitation by first training a robust regional accent classifier and then extending it through class-incremental learning. Our model also draws on the layer-wise structure of speech foundation models: prior work shows that different encoder layers capture different types of infor-mation, including acoustic, phonetic, speaker, and linguistic properties [8]. Since accent cues span pronunciation, phoneme realization, rhythm, and prosody, AccentCL aggregates repre-sentations across multiple Whisper encoder layers rather than relying on the final layer alone. 

B. Class-Incremental Learning 

Class-incremental learning addresses the problem of extend-ing a model with new classes while preserving performance on previously learned ones. Unlike task-incremental learning [12], the model must predict over a unified label space at test time, which makes this setting especially prone to catastrophic forgetting and bias toward recently added classes. Existing methods typically reduce forgetting through replay, which mixes stored old-class examples with new-class data, or through regularization and distillation, which encourage the updated model to stay close to the previous model [13]–[16]. This line of work is directly relevant to accent classification, since new accent categories may need to be introduced after a classifier has already been trained. However, fine-tuning directly on the new accent can bias the classifier toward that class and degrade previously learned accents. AccentCL supports incremental accent-class expansion by combining new-accent data with memory of previous accents drawn from multiple corpora. During training, it preserves the earlier model’s behavior on old-accent examples and suppresses the tendency to misclassify old accents as the newly added class, limiting degradation on earlier learned accents. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION 

We study accent classification as a robust class-incremental learning problem. Given an accent classifier trained on a set of “old” accent classes Yold , the goal is to add a “new” accent class ynew while preserving performance on the old classes. Before each incremental step, we construct a replay memory Dmem from the previously learned classes. During incremental training, the updated classifier fθ is trained on 

Dtrain = Dnew ∪ D mem , where Dnew contains training ut-terances from the newly added accent class. Each training example is represented as (xi, y i, d i), where xi is an utterance, 

yi ∈ Y is the accent label, and di is the domain/corpus label. The objective is to learn a function fθ that recognizes the new accent class, retains performance on old classes, and remains robust across heterogeneous speech corpora. IV. M ETHOD 

A. Phase 1: Base regional accent classifier 

The base classifier provides recognition capabilities on the “old” accents before expanding to new classes. We define an accent label space Ybase by collapsing the 16 fine-grained accent categories in CommonAccent [4] into 5 broad accent categories: North American (American and Canadian), British Isles (British, Irish, Scottish, and Welsh), Australasian (Aus-tralian and New Zealand), South Asian (Indian), and Southeast Asian (Malaysian and Singaporean). This grouping preserves broad regional structure while avoiding unreliable distinctions between fine-grained labels with limited training data.

a) Datasets: We construct our accent classification dataset from multiple English speech corpora, including Com-monVoice [11], GLOBE [20], AESRC [21], UK-Ireland [22], EdAcc [23], L2ARCTIC [24], ARCTIC [25], EmiliaYO-DAS [26], VoxPopuli [27], and EMIME [28]. We assign each utterance to a regional accent group following our collapsed accent label mapping. The full dataset includes North Ameri-can (NA), British Isles (BI), Australasian (AUS), South Asian (SA), Southeast Asian (SEA), Spanish (SPA), and Chinese (CHN) accent groups. All splits are constructed at the speaker level to prevent speaker overlap among the training, validation, and test sets. Because several corpora contain many utterances from the same speaker, we also limit speaker dominance during train-ing. For each accent group, we first cap each speaker at 50 utterances. We then limit the total number of utterances per accent group to 50,000. To preserve training dataset diversity, we sample across the datasets available for each accent group. This prevents the training subset from being dominated by a small number of frequent speakers or large corpora, while keeping variation in recording conditions across datasets. A. Robust regional accent classification 

Table I reports the performance of the 5-class base regional accent classifier. We evaluate each model on the full test set and on a source-held-out subset to assess generalization across recording sources. On the full evaluation set, AccentCL base achieves an accuracy of 76.0% , balanced accuracy of 77.1% ,and macro-F1 of 76.9% . Compared with the mapped Voxlect baseline, AccentCL base improves accuracy by +11.2 points, balanced accuracy by +11.3 points, and macro-F1 by +8.8 

points. These results show that the proposed regional classi-fier provides stronger overall and class-balanced performance under the shared five-class accent label space. On the source-held-out subset, AccentCL base achieves an OOD accuracy of 89.7% , balanced accuracy of 79.6% ,and macro-F1 of 83.0% . Compared with Voxlect, AccentCL improves OOD accuracy by +5.9 points, balanced accuracy by +2.1 points, and macro-F1 by +0.4 points. Although the OOD macro-F1 gain is modest, AccentCL maintains strong class-balanced performance while improving overall accuracy on held-out recording sources. Ablation results in Table I further show that both logit adjustment and DMA improve OOD robustness, with DMA having the larger individual effect and their combination providing the strongest generalization to unseen recording sources.

B. Class-incremental accent expansion 

We next evaluate whether the regional accent inventory can be expanded without retraining the full model from scratch. Starting from the five-class base model, we incrementally add two accent classes: first Spanish-accented English , and then Chinese-accented English , yielding a class-incremental sequence of 5 → 6 → 7 classes.

The retention-only variant gives the smallest forgetting in this step, suggesting that matching the previous classifier’s predictions helps preserve the performance of the original five regional classes. After adding Chinese-accented English, AccentCL again obtains the best new-class F1 ( 61.8 ) and old-class balanced accuracy ( 77.6% ). The margin-only variant has the smallest degradation on old classes in this step, indicating that reducing confusions between old accents and the new class is important when adding Chinese-accented English. Combining both gives ACCENT CL the most consistent balance between learning the new accent and maintaining performance on old accents. VII. D ISCUSSION 

We proposed A CCENT CL, a framework for English ac-cent classification that supports class-incremental expansion. By combining data from new accents with replay memory stratified by dataset, AccentCL adds Spanish- and Chinese-accented English without retraining on the full old dataset. In our setting, only about 10% of the old training data is retained as replay memory and still maintains balanced accuracy on old accent classes across incremental steps. The ablation results show that retention and old-to-new margin regularization provide complementary benefits. Re-tention preserves the previous classifier’s behavior while the margin loss reduces confusion between old accents and the newly added class. New-class F1, balanced accuracy on old classes, and worst-class forgetting further show that AccentCL balances learning new accents with retaining old accents. A limitation of the current study is that continual learning is evaluated over only two expansion steps, using a single class order and random seed. Future work will examine robustness across additional expansion sequences and random seeds. Compared with the Voxlect state-of-the-art baseline, Ac-centCL achieves stronger regional accent classification and better adaptation to the expanded label space. These results show that AccentCL provides a practical pipeline for continu-ally expanding accent classifiers to future accent categories without retraining from scratch or substantially degrading previously learned classes.
