# Abstract 

Text-to-speech needs written text in spoken form, and speech recognition output needs the reverse. For Khmer, neither direction has a maintained open-source tool, and the script makes both harder: words are not separated by spaces, and number words occur inside ordinary words. We present Tha , a Khmer text nor-malization and inverse text normalization toolkit built from weighted finite-state transducers. It segments and classifies a whole line in one shortest-path search, and a second transducer rejects token boundaries inside a Khmer syllable. On Google’s Khmer test suite, Tha 

agrees with the reference on all 274 cardinals up to one spelling variant, and on 2,906 real TTS prompts, 153 of the 158 sentences it rewrites are correct. Tha is open source under the Apache 2.0 license. 

# 1 Introduction 

A TTS front end has to turn “ តៃម្ល $1.05” into the words a speaker would say, “ តៃម្ល មួយ ដុលា្លរ្រ បាំ េសន ”, and an ASR system has to turn “ ពីររយហាសិបដុលា្លរ ” back into “$250”. These two tasks, text normalization (TN) and inverse text normalization (ITN), are usually done with hand-written grammars compiled into weighted finite-state transducers (WFSTs) [6, 10, 27], since neural mod-els still make the occasional unrecoverable error, such as reading the wrong number [25, 36]. For Khmer, the only released grammars are Google’s Thrax grammars for TTS [24]. They do TN only, ex-pect input that is already segmented into words, and have been archived since 2021. NeMo [37] and We-TextProcessing [32] do not support Khmer. The lan-guage also breaks some of their assumptions: Khmer is written without spaces between words, so there are no tokens to classify one at a time, and short number words occur inside ordinary words ( ពីរ , “two”, begins 

> ពីេរាះ

Tha is an open-source toolkit for both directions. It tags a whole line in one shortest-path search, so it needs no word segmenter (§4.1), and a filter transducer rejects tokens that start or end inside a Khmer syllable (§4.2). We describe the grammars for 17 TN and 13 ITN classes (§4.3, §4.5) and evaluate them against Google’s test suite, on spoken-form TTS prompts, by round trip and with an ablation (§5).

# 2 Khmer numbers and script 

Script. Khmer is an abugida of the Brahmic family. A syllable is a base consonant, optionally stacked with subscript consonants written with the invisible coeng 

sign (U+17D2), followed by dependent vowel signs and diacritics [13, 30]. Unicode allows several character or-ders for the same visible syllable, for instance a vowel typed before a subscript instead of after it, and fonts render them alike; SIL’s Khmer character specification defines a canonical order [22]. Spaces mark phrase boundaries rather than words, and the zero-width space (U+200B) is used inconsistently to mark word bound-aries [24]. The repetition mark ៗ repeats the preceding word ( េក្មងៗ , “children”, is read េក្មង េក្មង ), which requires knowing where that word begins. 

Number names. Table 1 lists the Khmer number words. Six to nine are compounds of five ( ្រ បាំ មួយ , “five-one”, is six), so ្រ បាំ បួន can be read as nine or as “five, four”. The tens have a spoken short form without សិប 

(សាម្របាំ for សាមសិប្របាំ , 35). The scale words are រយ (10 2),

Written conventions. Khmer text uses Khmer and Latin digits interchangeably, often in the same docu-ment. Thousands are separated by spaces, commas or dots ( ១.០០០ is usually one thousand, not one), decimals 1arXiv:2609.30984v1 [cs.CL] 25 Sep 2026 by dots or commas. Dates are written day first. A hy-phen between two numbers is read ដល់ (“to”) in a range, but ទល់ (“against”) in a sports score. These ambigui-ties cannot be resolved by a grammar of the token alone, and §4.3 describes the context rules Tha applies. 

# 3 Related work 

Grammar-based normalization. Text normaliza-tion as a task was laid out by Sproat et al. [27], with a taxonomy of semiotic classes later extended by van Esch and Sproat [31]. Production systems such as Kestrel [6] and its open-source counterpart Spar-rowhawk [8] split normalization into a tokenizer and classifier, which tags spans with a semiotic class and fields, and a verbalizer, which turns each tagged token into words. The grammars are written in Thrax [29] or Pynini [10] and compiled into WFSTs with OpenFst [1], using weighted rewrite rules [17] and shortest-path search [12, 16]. The same grammars can serve ASR and TTS [21]. NeMo [37] and WeTextProcessing [32] bring this design to Pynini with TN and ITN for many languages; Tha follows NeMo’s tagger and verbalizer interfaces. 

Neural and hybrid approaches. Sequence-to-sequence models normalize most tokens correctly but make a small number of unacceptable errors, such as reading the wrong number [25, 26]; covering grammars [36] and shallow fusion with a WFST [2] constrain them. Neural ITN has followed the same path [19, 28]. Large language models have recently been prompted for both TN [15, 33] and spoken-to-written conversion [4]. 

Southeast Asian languages. Sodimana et al. [24] wrote Thrax normalization grammars for Bangla, Khmer, Nepali, Javanese, Sinhala and Sundanese to build TTS voices [9, 23]. Their Khmer grammars expect input segmented with a CRF word segmenter [3] and leave segmentation errors to the grammars. Burmese, also written without spaces, has FST normal-ization grammars [18], and Nisaba provides finite-state script normalization for Brahmic scripts [14]. 

Khmer. Khmer is one of the evaluation languages of Gorman and Sproat [11], whose number grammars are induced from a few examples but cover cardinals only and were not released. Tha uses the khmer-cut word segmenter [34] only to expand the repetition mark (§4.4). Multilingual speech corpora and mod-els such as FLEURS and Whisper now cover Khmer [5, 20]. KhmerTagger [35] tags number spans and punc-tuation in ASR output with XLM-RoBERTa; Tha ’s ITN grammars could replace its rule-based converter for the tagged spans (§6).

Unlike the current up-stream version, it keeps coeng ដ and coeng ត apart, since they are pronounced differently. Google’s TTS prompts are already in canonical order (the cleanup changes none of them), so the evaluation in §5 does not depend on it.

## 4.4 The repetition mark 

The mark ៗ tells the reader to say the preceding word again: េក្មងៗ (“children”) is read េក្មង េក្មង . It does not say how much text the preceding word is. With no spaces between words, the scope has to be recovered, and it is not always one word: បន្តិចម្ដងៗ (“little by little”) repeats the two words បន្តិចម្ដង , and ពីរៃថ្ងម្ដងៗ (“every two days”) repeats three. 

Tha segments up to 120 characters before the mark with khmercut [34], on the same line and after the last space, because a space ends the phrase: in េរៀងរាល់ៃថ្ង ម្ដងៗ 

only ម្ដង is repeated. Of the last three words w1w2w3,the rules in Table 3 choose how many to repeat; by default it is the last one. The rules accept both spellings of ម្ដង , with coeng ដ or coeng ត. A space before the mark is ignored ( េក្មង ៗ ), and a mark with no word before it is dropped. The ITN post-processing reverses the expansion: a word or phrase repeated with gets its mark back.

Arithmetic. The operator words the normalizer pro-duces for +, −, ×, ÷ and = (បូក , ដក , គុណ , ែចក , េស្មី ) are written back as symbols when they stand between two numbers: ៃម្ភដកដប់ → 20 - 10, ពីរបូកបីេស្មី្របាំ → 2 + 3 = 5. Outside that position they stay words, so ែចករំែលក (“to share”) and គុណភាព (“quality”) are left alone. A single expression token is cheaper than two numbers around a word, so ៃម្ភដកដប់ is never read as 20 followed by −10 .

Tha is a Python package built on Pynini 2.1.7 [10]. The grammars compile when first used, in 4.7 s for TN and 1.5 s for ITN on a laptop, and are cached for the process. The compiled TN classifier has 425,482 states and 487,894 arcs, the ITN classifier 246,685 states and 352,104 arcs (Figure 4). The API mirrors NeMo’s:

# 5 Evaluation 

There is no public gold standard for Khmer text nor-malization, so we use four smaller evaluations that can all be rerun from public data: agreement with an inde-pendent grammar, ITN on real spoken-form text, round trips, and an ablation. 

Data. (1) The verbalizer test files of Google’s Khmer grammars [7, 24]: 274 cardinals, and decimals and times, each with a structured value and the words the reference grammar produces. (2) 2,906 prompts of Google’s Khmer TTS corpus [9, 23], real sentences already written in spoken form, with numbers as words. Both sources separate every word with a space, which Khmer text does not do, so we remove the spaces between Khmer characters before running ITN: the prompts then read like ordinary Khmer text, and ev-ery number has to be found without word boundaries. (3) The 62,175 Khmer entries of the accompanying pro-nunciation lexicon, as a list of ordinary words that ITN should leave alone. (4) Randomly generated written to-kens of ten classes, 300 per class.

Table 4 compares Tha with the reference verbalizations. In the TN direction, Tha ’s cardinal words equal the reference, spaces removed, in 236 of 274 cases. All other cases differ only in the spelling of seven: the reference writes ្រ បាំ ពិល , following the pronunciation, where Tha

In the ITN direction we read the reference’s spo-ken forms back with Tha ; single digits without a sign, which Tha deliberately leaves as words, are excluded. Before we added ្រ បាំ ពិល to the ITN grammar, every cardinal error came from this spelling. ដប់្របាំ ពិល (17), for example, was read as “15 ពិល ”, because the num-ber grammar matched ្រ បាំ (“five”) and the boundary filter cannot tell that ពិល continues the word. With the spelling added, Tha reads all cardinals and times cor-rectly. The four remaining decimal errors are numbers in scientific notation ( គុណនឹងដប់ស្វ័យគុណ… , “times ten to the power of”), which Tha does not support. We would not have found the ្រ បាំ ពិល problem with our own test cases, which all use the standard spelling.

## 5.4 Ablation 

We removed one design choice at a time and ran ITN over the lexicon entries and the prompts (Figure 7). Most lexicon entries are single words and should come back unchanged. We classify every change automati-cally by aligning the number’s span with khmercut’s word boundaries: the whole entry is a number ( ដប់ ); a number sits at a word boundary of a compound entry, such as a place name ( ភ្នំមួយរយ → ភ្នំ 100) or an ordinal (ទីបី → ទី 3), which is a legitimate reading; or a number starts or ends inside a word, which is an error. With every rule on, Tha changes 36 of the 62,175 entries, and the proxy flags 4 as inside a word. ## 5.5 Speed 

Figure 8 shows the time per sentence over the prompts, on one core of a laptop (Apple silicon). The median prompt takes 8.6 ms to inverse normalize and its writ-ten form 3.4 ms to normalize (95th percentiles 17.8 and 6.8 ms), about 4.4 and 10.9 thousand characters per sec-ond. Time grows linearly with length, as expected of composition with a fixed transducer. 

# 6 Discussion and limitations 

Ambiguity. A better grammar will not fix every er-ror. A telephone number read in chunks, េផាន as cur-rency or weight, a dash as range or score, and ដក before a count ( ដកដប់នាក់េចញ , “take ten people out”, is written 

−10 នាក់េចញ ) all need information that is not in the to-ken. Tha resolves the last with sentence-level cues and leaves the first two to its weights; a language model rescoring the lattice, as in shallow fusion [2], or a neu-ral span tagger such as KhmerTagger [35] could supply the missing context while Tha ’s grammars keep the conversion itself exact. 

Orthographic variation. The Google evaluation and the TTS prompts both show that the errors that re-main come from spellings the grammar does not know: pronunciation spellings ( ្រ បាំ ពិល ), misspelled units and typos. Style choices. Several outputs are conventions rather than facts: Latin or Khmer digits, symbols ( ៛, %) or unit words, numeric or spelled dates, and whether single digits become digits. Tha exposes the most im-portant as options, but a downstream user may prefer others. 

Coverage. ITN does not read back Roman numerals, letter codes ( ជី is both the letter G and “fertilizer”) or whitelist abbreviations. TN has no abbreviation expan-sion beyond a short whitelist. 

Evaluation. Our evaluation uses an independent grammar’s test suite, one corpus of spoken-form text, round trips and an ablation; it does not include a large, independently annotated gold standard of writ-ten Khmer text, nor a measurement of the repetition rules of §4.4, or a comparison with neural or LLM-based normalizers [33, 36]. Building such a test set is the most useful next step. 

# 7 Conclusion 

Tha brings the tagger and verbalizer design of Spar-rowhawk and NeMo to Khmer. Tagging the whole line at once removes the need for a word segmenter, and the boundary filter is what allows ITN to run over raw text without breaking ordinary words. The main open prob-lems are spelling variation and a proper annotated test set. The code, grammars, experiment scripts and re-sults are available at https://github.com/seanghay/ tha .
